CHAPTER 2The E–k Diagram, Effective Mass, Holes

A map that tells you how an electron behaves inside a crystal, and why an electron in silicon acts as if it had a different mass.

Your notes p.4 to p.7

2.1 What are k and the E–k diagram?

Explain like I'm 10

You can describe a song in two ways: as the wiggly sound in time, or as a list of which notes (frequencies) it contains. Both describe the same song. The switch between them is called a Fourier transform. Electrons are waves too. We can describe them by where they are (real space, in cm) or by how fast the wave wiggles in space (reciprocal space, in cm-1). That second number is k, the wave-vector. More wiggles per centimetre = bigger k = faster electron.

Real domainFourier transform →Reciprocal domain
time (s)F.T.frequency ω
real space (cm)F.T.reciprocal space (cm-1)
wavelength λ (cm)F.T.k = 2π/λ (cm-1), the wave-vector

Derivation from your notes: ħk is momentum

Momentum of the electron wave
ħk = ħ · [[2π | λ]] = [[h | 2π]] · [[2π | λ]] = [[h | λ]] = p   ⇒  p = ħk
(ħ = h/2π is the reduced Planck constant; h/λ is the de Broglie momentum.)

Free-electron energy

Kinetic energy → parabola
K.E. = ½ m v² = [[p² | 2m]]  ⇒  E = [[ħ²k² | 2m]]

So for a lone electron E versus k is a parabola (left plot below). For an electron in a crystal, the periodic atoms cut the parabola into pieces and open gaps exactly at k = ±π/a, ±2π/a, … (a = lattice spacing). Those gaps are the band gaps (right plot).

{{fig:ek_free_crystal|Your page 5. Left: free electron. Right: a crystal lattice chops the parabola and opens forbidden gaps at ±π/a, ±2π/a.}}
Beyond your notes: why gaps appear at ±π/a

An electron wave whose wavelength is exactly twice the lattice spacing (λ = 2a, so k = π/a) gets reflected by each layer of atoms (Bragg reflection). Reflected and forward waves combine into two standing waves: one piles up charge on the atoms (lower energy) and one between the atoms (higher energy). The energy difference between them is the gap.

2.2 Effective mass m*

Near the bottom of a band the crystal E–k curve still looks like a parabola, but a different one. We keep the free-electron formula and just swap the mass:

Effective-mass parabola
E = [[ħ²k² | 2m*]]  where m* = effective mass of the electron

Getting m* from the curve (your derivation)

E = [[ħ²k² | 2m*]]  ⇒  [[dE | dk]] = [[ħ²k | m*]]  ⇒  [[d²E | dk²]] = [[ħ² | m*]]
m* = [[ħ² | d²E/dk²]]  ← inverse of the curvature of the E–k diagram
Explain like I'm 10

Pushing a shopping cart through a quiet hall is easy. Pushing the same cart through a crowded market is hard because people keep bumping it. The cart hasn't gotten heavier; the crowd is just interfering. An electron in a crystal is being pushed and pulled by all the atoms, so it responds to a force as if it had a new mass, the effective mass. Steep, sharply curved E–k = light and nimble electron. Flat, lazy E–k = heavy, sluggish electron.

Remember
high curvature ⇒ low m* ⇒ high mobility ⇒ high current density
low curvature (flat) ⇒ high m* ⇒ low mobility ⇒ low current density
Your notes' question: two semiconductors have the E–k curves below. Which has the higher current density?

Semiconductor 1's curve is sharper (higher curvature) than semiconductor 2's. So:

  1. Curvature ↑ ⇒ m* = ħ²/(d²E/dk²) ↓.
  2. Since μ = qτ/m*, a smaller m* gives a higher mobility.
  3. J = nqμE, so a higher μ gives a higher current density (same n, same E).

Answer: semiconductor 1 (the higher-curvature one).

Careful with the numbers

Your page also shows m* = 0.2m0 and m* = 0.06m0 (struck through). If a question gives numbers, compare them directly: the smaller m* always wins. With those two values the 0.06m0 material has the higher mobility, so it would have to be the one drawn with the higher curvature. Always match the curve to its mass before answering. (m0 = 9.11×10-31 kg is the free-electron mass.)

Extra: an E–k curve is E = 2×10-38 k² (E in J, k in m-1). Find m* in terms of m0.

Compare with E = ħ²k²/2m*: ħ²/2m* = 2×10-38. With ħ = 1.055×10-34 J·s, ħ² = 1.113×10-68. So m* = ħ²/(2 × 2×10-38) = 1.113×10-68 / 4×10-38 = 2.78×10-31 kg ≈ 0.31 m0.

2.3 Holes

Your notes say

A hole is the absence of an electron, modelled as a positively charged particle. It always moves in the valence band. mh* = effective mass of holes, me* = effective mass of electrons.

Explain like I'm 10

A parking lot is completely full except for one empty space. When a car moves into the empty space, a new empty space appears where it came from, so the empty space seems to travel backwards. Tracking the one empty space is much easier than tracking 100 cars. The moving empty space is the hole. It acts like a tiny positive charge that moves in the direction of the electric field, while electrons go the opposite way.

  • When an electron jumps from the VB to the CB it leaves a hole behind: an electron–hole pair (EHP) is created.
  • Electrons in the CB and holes in the VB both carry current.
  • In a hole picture the valence-band E–k curve is turned upside down (a hill), and the hole's effective mass is given the positive sign.
  • In Si, mh* > me* (holes are heavier), so holes are slower: μp < μn.

2.4 Ec, Ev and Eg on the E–k diagram

Ec = lowest point of the conduction band, Ev = highest point of the valence band, and Eg = Ec − Ev. Your notes draw the CB as an upward parabola sitting on top of the VB's downward parabola.

Explain like I'm 10

A phonon is a little shake of the crystal's atoms (heat vibration). A photon (light particle) carries plenty of energy but almost no momentum, like a ping-pong ball. Jumping straight up is easy. But in silicon the top floor's doorway is off to the side, so the electron needs a sideways shove as well. The atom vibration (phonon) provides that sideways shove, like a friend giving you a push at the same moment you jump. Needing two things to happen together makes silicon bad at making light.

Exam traps
  • Photons carry energy but negligible momentum; phonons carry momentum but little energy. That's why indirect transitions need both.
  • Silicon is indirect. Many students memorise "Si = direct" by mistake because it is the most common material.

CHAPTER 3Fermi–Dirac Statistics, Fermi Level, Density of States

How many electrons are actually sitting on the top floor? Two ingredients: how many seats exist, and how likely each seat is to be taken.

Your notes p.7 to p.9, p.11 to p.12

3.1 The Fermi–Dirac function f(E)

Probability that a state of energy E is occupied by an electron
f(E) = [[1 | 1 + exp( (E − EF) / kBT )]]
1 − f(E) = probability the state is empty = probability of finding a hole
Explain like I'm 10

Imagine a huge cinema where the seats are the energy levels. The Fermi level EF is an invisible "50-50 line": a seat exactly at that height is taken half the time. Seats far below the line are nearly always taken; seats far above are nearly always empty. The temperature decides how blurry the line is. At absolute zero the line is razor sharp; when it is warm, a few kids from the lower rows jump to seats above the line.

Important: the Fermi level does not have to be a real seat. Your notes call it an "imaginary construct". It can sit in the forbidden gap where no seat exists.

{{fig:fermi|Your pages 7, 12: at T = 0 K a perfect step; as T rises the step smooths. Every curve passes through f = 0.5 at E = E_F.}}

The three cases your notes list at T = 0 K

Energyf(E) at T = 0 KMeaning
E < EF1every state below EF is completely filled
E = EF½ (50%)the borderline
E > EF0every state above EF is completely empty

Why? For T → 0, if E < EF the exponent (E−EF)/kBT → −∞, so e−∞ = 0 and f = 1/(1+0) = 1. If E > EF the exponent → +∞, e+∞ = ∞, so f = 0.

Your notes, Q1: At T = 300 K find the probability of occupation of a state at (a) E = EF + 0.1 eV, (b) E = EF − 0.1 eV.
  1. Thermal energy kBT ≈ 0.026 eV at 300 K.
  2. (a) Exponent = 0.1/0.026 = 3.846, e3.846 = 46.81. So f = 1/(1 + 46.81) = 0.0209 = 2.09 %.
  3. (b) Exponent = −3.846, e−3.846 = 0.0213. So f = 1/(1 + 0.0213) = 0.979 = 97.9 %.

Check: the two answers add to 100 %. This always happens: f(EF+Δ) + f(EF−Δ) = 1. The chance a state Δ above EF is filled equals the chance a state Δ below is empty. (With kBT = 0.02585 eV you get 2.05 % and 97.95 %; both are accepted.)

Your notes, Q2: Plot f(E) versus E at T = 0 K, 200 K, 300 K.

Draw the same axes for all three. At 0 K: a horizontal line at 1 dropping vertically to 0 at EF. At 200 K: a fairly steep S-curve through (EF, 0.5). At 300 K: a gentler S-curve, also through (EF, 0.5). The hotter the curve, the more its "shoulders" spread out, so the more states above EF are occupied and the more below are empty. See the figure above (the extra 600 K curve shows the trend continuing).

Extra: at what energy above EF is f = 1 % at 300 K?

0.01 = 1/(1+ex) ⇒ ex = 99 ⇒ x = ln 99 = 4.595. E − EF = 4.595 × 0.02585 = 0.119 eV. In general, f ≈ e−(E−EF)/kBT once E − EF ≫ kBT (the "Boltzmann tail").

3.2 Density of states g(E)

Your notes say

g(E) = number of energy states available per unit energy per unit volume. f(E) = probability that a state is occupied.

Explain like I'm 10

g(E) is how many seats exist at each height. f(E) is the chance each seat is occupied. To count how many kids are actually in the top-floor seats, multiply "seats at each height" by "chance each is taken" and add up all heights. That is what the integral does.

Electron concentration in the conduction band
n = ∫Ec∞ g(E) f(E) dE    (similarly p = ∫−∞Ev g(E)[1 − f(E)] dE)
Beyond your notes: the results of that integral

Near the band edges g(E) ∝ √(E − Ec) (3-D crystal). Using the Boltzmann tail of f, the integral gives:

n = Nc exp[ −(Ec − EF)/kBT ]    p = Nv exp[ −(EF − Ev)/kBT ]
n = ni exp[ (EF − Ei)/kBT ]    p = ni exp[ (Ei − EF)/kBT ]

Nc, Nv are the "effective densities of states" (about 2.8×1019 and 1.04×1019 cm-3 for Si at 300 K); Ei is the intrinsic (mid-gap) Fermi level. The message: the closer EF is to Ec, the more electrons; the closer to Ev, the more holes. Multiplying the two gives n·p = NcNve−Eg/kT = ni², the mass-action law of Chapter 5.

Your notes say

As you increase the doping, EF shifts towards the conduction band for n-type and towards the valence band for p-type.

Explain like I'm 10

EF is like the water level in a tank next to two shelves. Add donors (extra electrons) and the level rises toward the top shelf (CB). Add acceptors (extra holes) and the level sinks toward the bottom shelf (VB). The water level tells you at a glance which carrier is the majority.

3.4 Reading band diagrams (very common exam question)

  1. Flat EF everywhere ⇒ the system is at equilibrium ⇒ no net current.
  2. Sloped EF ⇒ current is flowing through the device (not equilibrium).
  3. Sloped (tilted) Ec and Ev ⇒ an electric field exists. The steeper the tilt, the stronger the field.
  4. Electric field: E = (1/q) dEc/dx (derived in Chapter 7). Electrons roll downhill in energy on the diagram; holes float uphill.
