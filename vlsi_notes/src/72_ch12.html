12.1 Structure: pull-up and pull-down

{{fig:cmos_switch|Left: the CMOS inverter from your notes. Middle and right: its switch model (your photo), with each ON transistor replaced by a resistor.}}
  • M2 = pMOS, source to VDD: the pull-up network (PUN). It can connect the output to logic 1.
  • M1 = nMOS, source to GND: the pull-down network (PDN). It can connect the output to logic 0.
  • The two gates are tied together to Vin; the two drains are tied together to Vout; Cload hangs from Vout to ground.
  • Logic levels: 0 = GND, 1 = VDD. This is static CMOS technology.
  • Device reminders from your notes: the nMOS is an n-channel enhancement MOSFET, ON for VGS > Vtn (positive). The pMOS is a p-channel enhancement MOSFET (p⁺ source/drain in an n-substrate or n-well), ON for VGS < Vtp (negative), i.e. when its gate is at least |Vtp| below its source.

Explain like I'm 10

A see-saw with two doors. One door opens to a room full of "1"s (VDD), the other to a room full of "0"s (ground). The input pushes the see-saw: push down (input 0) and the top door opens, so the output fills up to 1. Push up (input 1) and the bottom door opens, so the output drains to 0. Because the see-saw can never have both doors open at rest, water never pours straight from the top room to the bottom room. That's why CMOS wastes almost no power when it is just sitting there.

12.2 The switch model and delay

Your photo and the Drive replace each transistor with a switch in series with its on-resistance (Chapter 11, Ron):

  • Vin = 0: pMOS = resistor Rp from VDD to Vout; nMOS = open switch. CL charges up through Rp.
  • Vin = VDD: pMOS open; nMOS = resistor Rn to ground. CL discharges through Rn.

Your notes say "you can calculate the time constant": each case is a first-order RC circuit (the Drive: "first-order ODE").

Delay of the inverter
Discharge: Vout(t) = VDD e−t/RnCL    time constant τn = RnCL (high to low)
Charge: Vout(t) = VDD(1 − e−t/RpCL)    τp = RpCL (low to high)
Time to reach 50 %: e−t/τ = ½ ⇒ t = τ ln 2 = 0.69τ  ⇒  tpHL = 0.69RnCL, tpLH = 0.69RpCL, tp = (tpHL + tpLH)/2

What is CL? (Drive) The internal capacitances of the inverter itself (the gate–drain and drain–body capacitances of both transistors), the wiring, and the input (gate) capacitance of every gate it drives.

Worked: Rn = 5 kΩ, Rp = 10 kΩ, CL = 20 fF. Find tpHL, tpLH, tp. How should the pMOS be resized to equalise them?
  1. tpHL = 0.69 × 5 kΩ × 20 fF = 69 ps.
  2. tpLH = 0.69 × 10 kΩ × 20 fF = 138 ps.
  3. tp = (69 + 138)/2 = 103.5 ps.
  4. R ∝ L/W, so doubling the pMOS width halves Rp to 5 kΩ and makes tpLH = 69 ps (ignoring the extra capacitance the wider pMOS adds).

12.3 Static behaviour: the three properties in your notes

  1. Zero (very small) static power dissipation. In either steady state one transistor is OFF, so there is no DC path from VDD to GND. Only tiny leakage (sub-threshold, junction) flows.
  2. High input impedance. The input drives only gates, and the gate oxide is an insulator: no DC input current. Hence the Drive's static fan-out is ideally infinite: one inverter could hold any number of following gates at the right DC level. But every extra gate adds capacitance, so the dynamic fan-out is limited by speed.
  3. Low output impedance. The output is always connected to VDD or GND through an ON transistor (a few kΩ). It is "stiff": noise injected onto the output is quickly pulled back, giving good noise immunity.

The Drive adds a fourth: ratioless logic with full (rail-to-rail) swing. VOH = VDD and VOL = 0 exactly, whatever the W/L of the two transistors. (Sizing changes speed and the switching point, not the final levels.) The pseudo-nMOS gate of §12.7 is ratioed: its VOL depends on the size ratio.

Switching threshold VM

Your notes: VM is where the VTC crosses the line Vout = Vin. At that point both transistors are in saturation, so equate the two saturation currents with Vin = Vout = VM:

  1. ½kn(VM − Vtn)² = ½kp(VDD − VM − |Vtp|)², where kn = μnCox(W/L)n, kp = μpCox(W/L)p.
  2. Take square roots with r = √(kp/kn): VM − Vtn = r(VDD − VM − |Vtp|).
  3. Solve: VM = [[Vtn + r(VDD − |Vtp|)|1 + r]]

If kn = kp (r = 1) and Vtn = |Vtp|, then VM = VDD/2: a symmetric inverter. Since μn ≈ 2 to 3 μp, this needs (W/L)p = (μn/μp)(W/L)n ≈ 2 to 3 × (W/L)n. A stronger nMOS pulls VM down; a stronger pMOS pushes it up.

Worked: VDD = 1.8 V, Vtn = |Vtp| = 0.4 V, μnCox = 200 μA/V², μpCox = 80 μA/V², (W/L)n = 2. Find VM for (W/L)p = 2, and the (W/L)p for VM = 0.9 V.
  1. kn = 400 μA/V²; kp = 160 μA/V² ⇒ r = √0.4 = 0.632.
  2. VM = (0.4 + 0.632 × 1.4)/1.632 = (0.4 + 0.885)/1.632 = 0.79 V (below VDD/2: the nMOS is stronger).
  3. For VM = VDD/2 we need r = 1, kp = kn: 80 × (W/L)p = 400 ⇒ (W/L)p = 5 = 2.5 × (W/L)n.

Noise margins

Real signals carry noise (the Drive lists 1/f, shot, wiring and junction noise). The inverter must still read a slightly wrong "0" as 0 and a slightly wrong "1" as 1. The boundaries are the two points where the VTC slope is −1:

Noise margins
VIL = largest input still read as 0    VIH = smallest input read as 1    (both at dVout/dVin = −1)
NML = VIL − VOL     NMH = VOH − VIH    (CMOS: VOH = VDD, VOL = 0)
Beyond your notes: closed forms for a matched inverter (kn = kp, Vtn = |Vtp| = Vt)
VIL = [[3VDD + 2Vt|8]]    VIH = [[5VDD − 2Vt|8]]    NML = NMH = [[3VDD + 2Vt|8]]

Derivation sketch for VIL: in region B the nMOS is saturated and the pMOS linear. Write ½k(Vin − Vt)² = k[(VDD − Vin − Vt)(VDD − Vout) − ½(VDD − Vout)²], differentiate with respect to Vin, set dVout/dVin = −1, and solve the two equations together. VIH follows by symmetry. Example: VDD = 1.8 V, Vt = 0.4 V ⇒ VIL = 0.775 V, VIH = 1.025 V, both margins 0.775 V (43 % of VDD).

Gain

Gain Av = dVout/dVin is the slope of the VTC, largest (most negative) at VM. Your photo example: Av = 100, so a 10-3 V change at the input gives ΔVout = 100 × 10-3 = 0.1 V (in magnitude; the output moves the opposite way). The Drive's point: high gain makes the transition sharp, which gives large noise margins and fast regeneration of degraded logic levels; biased at VM, the inverter is also an analog amplifier.

12.5 Power dissipation

CMOS power
Dynamic: each 0 → 1 → 0 cycle takes CLVDD² from the supply (½CLVDD² burnt in the pMOS while charging, ½ stored then burnt in the nMOS while discharging) ⇒ Pdyn = α CL VDD² f (α = activity factor, fraction of cycles that switch)
Short-circuit: while Vin is between Vtn and VDD − |Vtp|, both devices conduct (the spike in the right panel of the figure). Small if input edges are fast.
Static: leakage only, Pstat = IleakVDD (sub-threshold, Chapter 11, plus junction leakage).

Because Pdyn ∝ VDD², lowering the supply is the most powerful way to save power; that is why supplies fell from 5 V to about 1 V.

Worked: an inverter drives 50 fF at 500 MHz with VDD = 1.8 V and switches every cycle (α = 1). Find the dynamic power. What if VDD is cut to 1.2 V?

P = 50×10-15 × 1.8² × 5×108 = 50×10-15 × 3.24 × 5×108 = 81 μW. At 1.2 V: × (1.2/1.8)² = × 0.444 ⇒ 36 μW (56 % less).

12.6 Sizing for equal rise and fall (board notes)

The board notes define tPLH (output 0 → 1) and tPHL (1 → 0) and design for tPLH = tPHL. Equal delays need equal resistances, i.e. equal current drive:

μnCox(W/L)n = μpCox(W/L)p  ⇒  (W/L)p = [[μn|μp]] (W/L)n ≈ 2 to 3 × (W/L)n

This is the same condition as VM = VDD/2 above: a symmetric inverter is symmetric in both its VTC and its timing.

12.7 Static CMOS gates: the design method

{{fig:cmos_gates|The gates designed on your board: NAND, NOR, the complex gate Y = (AB + CD)′, and pseudo-nMOS.}}
The method from your board notes
  1. Design the PDN (nMOS) from the un-inverted function: an OR (A + B) = nMOS in parallel; an AND (AB) = nMOS in series. The PDN conducts (pulls Y to 0) exactly when the function inside the bar is 1.
  2. The PUN (pMOS) is the dual: every parallel connection in the PDN becomes series in the PUN, and every series becomes parallel. The same input drives one nMOS and one pMOS.
  3. The output is the inverted function. Static CMOS can only make inverting gates directly (NAND, NOR, AOI, OAI). An AND = NAND + inverter.

An N-input static CMOS gate uses 2N transistors. At every input combination exactly one of PUN/PDN conducts, so the gate keeps all the inverter's properties: no static current and full swing.

PDN: M3 (A) ∥ M4 (B). PUN: M1 (A) in series with M2 (B).

Example 3 (board): Y = (AB + CD)′. PDN: (A series B) in parallel with (C series D). PUN, the dual: (A ∥ B) in series with (C ∥ D). 8 transistors. Check: A = B = 1 turns on the A–B nMOS chain ⇒ Y = 0; at the same time pMOS A and B are both off, so the upper PUN block is open ⇒ no path to VDD. ✓

Pseudo-nMOS technique (board): replace the whole PUN with a single pMOS whose gate is grounded (always ON) and keep the nMOS PDN. A 2-input NOR then needs 3 transistors instead of 4 (N + 1 instead of 2N). The cost: whenever the PDN is ON, current flows straight from VDD to GND through the always-on pMOS (static power), and VOL is not 0 but set by the pMOS/nMOS resistance ratio (ratioed logic). The 4×4 NOR ROM in Chapter 16 uses exactly this pull-up.

Beyond your notes: sizing gates for equal delay

Series transistors add their resistances. To make a NAND as fast as a reference inverter with (W/L)n = 1 and (W/L)p = 2.5, the two series nMOS must each be twice as wide (W/L = 2); the parallel pMOS can stay at 2.5 (worst case is only one of them ON). For a NOR, the two series pMOS must each be 5. Large series pMOS stacks are slow and big, which is why NAND gates are preferred over NOR gates in CMOS.

Practice: design Y = (A(B + C))′. Give the PDN, the PUN, the transistor count and the output for A = 1, B = 0, C = 1.
  1. Inside the bar: A AND (B OR C). PDN: nMOS A in series with (nMOS B ∥ nMOS C).
  2. PUN (dual): pMOS A in parallel with (pMOS B in series with pMOS C).
  3. 6 transistors (3 inputs × 2).
  4. A = 1, B = 0, C = 1: nMOS A on and nMOS C on ⇒ path to GND ⇒ Y = 0. Check the PUN: pMOS A off, and the B–C series pair is broken because pMOS C is off ⇒ no path to VDD. ✓ (A(B + C) = 1 ⇒ Y = 0.)
Practice: how many transistors does a static CMOS 3-input NOR need? Which network has the series stack, and why is it the slow one?

6 transistors. The PUN has three pMOS in series (the PDN has three nMOS in parallel). Series resistances add, and pMOS already has the lower mobility, so the 0 → 1 output transition through three series pMOS is the slowest. To compensate each pMOS would need to be about 3 × 2.5 = 7.5 times the unit nMOS width.

Exam traps
  • pMOS is the pull-up (to VDD); nMOS is the pull-down. pMOS passes a strong 1, nMOS passes a strong 0.
  • VM is where Vout = Vin, not where Vout = VDD/2 (they coincide only for a symmetric inverter).
  • In the PDN, AND ⇒ series, OR ⇒ parallel. The PUN is the dual, not a copy.
  • "Zero static power" means no DC current at rest. Power is still burnt every time the output switches: αCVDD²f.
  • Noise margins use the slope = −1 points, not the threshold VM.
  • Delay to 50 % is 0.69RC, not RC (RC is the 63 % time).
