CHAPTER 0The Big Picture: What Is VLSI?

Before any formulas: what are we even building, and why do we start with tiny bits of crystal?

Your notes p.1 (header list)
Explain like I'm 10

Imagine building a huge city out of LEGO. If you glue each brick by hand, one at a time, a city with a billion bricks would take forever. An integrated circuit (IC) is like a machine that prints the whole LEGO city at once on a piece of sand the size of your fingernail. VLSI (Very Large Scale Integration) just means the city has a very huge number of tiny buildings (transistors).

A transistor is a tiny switch. Millions of switches, wired together, can add numbers, remember things and run games. To make good switches we need a special material that can be a wire or a wall whenever we choose: that material is a semiconductor. That is why the whole course starts with semiconductors.

Discrete vs Integrated

  • Discrete circuit: separate components (resistors, transistors, capacitors) soldered onto a board with wires. Big, slow, expensive to build in large numbers.
  • Integrated circuit (IC): all those components made together, on one small piece of silicon, in one manufacturing process. Small, fast, cheap, low power.

Beyond your notes

Exact boundaries differ by textbook (some count transistors, some count gates). If a question asks "define VLSI", say "more than about 104 to 106 transistors/gates on a single chip" and mention the trend: the number of transistors per chip roughly doubles every two years (Moore's law).

The story in one breath

Crystals have energy "floors" (bands) → electrons and holes live on those floors → we control how many there are by adding impurities (doping) → they move by pushing (drift) and by spreading out (diffusion) → put a p-piece next to an n-piece and you get a one-way gate (diode) → stack three pieces and a small current controls a big one (BJT) → or use an electric field through an insulator to open and close a channel (MOSFET) → pair two kinds of MOSFET and you get a switch that uses almost no power when idle (CMOS), the building block of VLSI.

CHAPTER 1Semiconductors and Energy Bands

What makes a material a metal, an insulator or a semiconductor? The answer is a picture of energy shelves.

Your notes p.1 to p.3

1.1 What is a semiconductor?

Your notes say

A semiconductor is a material whose conductivity can be tuned by many orders of magnitude. Applications: (i) switch, (ii) amplifier, (iii) logic device, (iv) LED, solar cells, photodetectors, (v) the whole electronics industry.

Explain like I'm 10

Copper is like a wide-open highway: cars (electrons) run freely. Glass is like a highway with a giant wall across it: nothing gets through. A semiconductor is a highway with a small gate. If you give it a tiny push (heat, light, a little chemical "seasoning" called doping, or a voltage) the gate opens. Because we decide when it opens, we can make switches and amplifiers.

Typical conductivity numbers (in S/cm), so you can feel "many orders of magnitude":

Material typeConductivity σ (S/cm)Example
Metal104 to 106copper, aluminium
Semiconductor10-8 to 103 (tunable!)silicon: from about 4×10-6 pure to 103 heavily doped
Insulatorbelow 10-12glass, SiO2

1.2 Elemental vs compound semiconductors

Elemental

Made of one kind of atom, from group IV of the periodic table.

Examples: Si (silicon), Ge (germanium).

Compound

Made of two or more elements, e.g. group III + group V.

Examples: GaAs (gallium arsenide), GaN (gallium nitride).

Beyond your notes: crystal structure

Your Unit-1 list starts with "crystal structure". Silicon and germanium atoms sit in a diamond cubic lattice: every atom has 4 nearest neighbours and shares one electron with each (a covalent bond), so every atom "feels" it has 8 outer electrons. GaAs uses the very similar zinc-blende lattice (Ga and As alternate). A crystal means the atoms repeat in a perfectly regular pattern; this regularity is what creates energy bands.

1.3 Energy bands: valence band, conduction band, band gap

Explain like I'm 10

Think of a two-storey building for electrons. The ground floor is the valence band (VB): it is crowded, everybody is packed in and cannot move around. The top floor is the conduction band (CB): it is empty and roomy, so anyone who gets up there can run about and carry electricity. Between the floors there is empty space where nobody can stand: the energy band gap Eg. To climb from ground floor to top floor an electron needs at least Eg of energy (from heat or light).

Your notes say

Metals: the VB and CB touch/overlap. Insulators: Eg "very greater". Semiconductors: Eg "lesser". Eg = energy difference between the lowest level of the CB and the highest level of the VB.

1.4 Where do bands come from? (Formation of energy bands)

Your notes use carbon as the example (silicon behaves the same way, just one shell down).

Explain like I'm 10

One lonely atom has its own private "rooms" (energy levels): exact, single rooms. Now bring many atoms close together. Electrons from neighbouring atoms start to influence each other, and the rules of quantum physics say two electrons can't have exactly the same energy. So the single room of every atom is nudged a tiny bit up or down from its neighbours', and a huge crowd of almost-identical rooms forms a smooth-looking band, like a stairway so finely stepped that it looks like a ramp.

Step by step with carbon (Z = 6)

  1. Electron configuration: 1s2 2s2 2p2. Inner 1s stays tightly bound; the action is in the outer shell (2s and 2p).
  2. Number of states: an s-level has 2 states (spin up/down), a p-level has 6 states (three orbitals × 2 spins). Your notes list 1s: 2, 2s: 2, 2p: 6.
  3. Electrons available: 2s holds 2 electrons, 2p holds only 2 of its 6 states. So each atom has 4 outer electrons and 8 outer states (2 + 6).
  4. For N atoms: 8N states and 4N electrons. (Your notes: "6N states, 2N electrons" for p, "2N states, 2N electrons" for s.)
  5. As the atoms come closer, the 2s level and 2p level spread into bands, then merge and re-split into two bands of 4N states each. The lower one holds all 4N electrons: full = valence band. The upper one is empty = conduction band. The empty region between them is Eg.
{{fig:band_formation|Your notes page 3: the 2s and 2p bands from N atoms; at the real crystal spacing they leave 4N filled states below and 4N empty states above.}}
Counting rule
N atoms → 8N outer states, 4N outer electrons → VB: 4N states (full), CB: 4N states (empty at 0 K)

GaN's big gap is why it emits blue light and handles high voltages; Ge's tiny gap is why Ge devices leak a lot and turn on at lower voltage.

1.6 Thermal energy of an electron

Heat gives electrons random kicks. The typical kick size is kBT.

Thermal energy
Ethermal = kBT    with kB = 1.38×10-23 J/K (Boltzmann constant)
At T = 300 K: E = [[300 × 1.38×10-23 | 1.6×10-19]] ≈ 25.87 meV ≈ 26 meV ≈ 0.026 eV

The division by 1.6×10-19 converts joules to electron-volts (1 eV = 1.6×10-19 J).

Explain like I'm 10

Silicon's gap is 1.1 eV but a typical heat kick is only 0.026 eV, roughly 42 times too small. So heat alone almost never lifts an electron across the gap, and only a rare, lucky electron makes it. That is why pure silicon at room temperature is such a poor conductor, and why doping is needed.

Worked question. How many times bigger than kBT at 300 K is the silicon band gap? What if T = 600 K?

Eg/kBT = 1.1 / 0.02585 ≈ 42.6 at 300 K. At 600 K, kBT doubles to 0.0517 eV, so the ratio is 1.1/0.0517 ≈ 21.3. The exponent in ni ∝ e-Eg/2kBT halves, so ni grows enormously with temperature.

Exam traps for this chapter
  • Don't say "electrons are in the band gap". The gap is forbidden: no allowed states.
  • Metals have no gap because bands overlap, not because the gap is zero-sized in a semiconductor sense.
  • Always convert: 1 eV = 1.6×10-19 J. Forgetting this gives answers off by 1019.
