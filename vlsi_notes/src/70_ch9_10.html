CHAPTER 9The MOS Capacitor

The heart of a MOSFET is a capacitor whose bottom plate is a semiconductor. Understand its three states and you understand how a transistor switches on.

Your second note-set, p.1 to p.3

9.1 The family tree: MOS → MOSFET → CMOS

  • MOS = Metal–Oxide–Semiconductor structure (a capacitor).
  • MOSFET = MOS Field-Effect Transistor: MOS capacitor + two n⁺ or p⁺ regions (source and drain).
  • CMOS = Complementary MOS: an nMOS and a pMOS working as a pair. The technology of essentially all modern VLSI chips.

9.2 Ordinary capacitor vs MOS capacitor

{{fig:capacitors|Your page 1: the MIM system vs the MIS (MOS) system.}}

MIM (metal–insulator–metal)

Both plates are metals. C = ε₀A/d never changes, so C/A = ε/d is a constant.

MIS / MOS (metal–insulator–semiconductor)

Top plate: the gate metal. Insulator: silicon dioxide, SiO2. Bottom plate: a p-type or n-type semiconductor. C is not constant; it depends on the gate voltage VG, because the semiconductor plate can change how deep its charge sits.

Explain like I'm 10

A metal plate is like a solid wall: if you put charge on it, the charge sits right at the surface. A semiconductor plate is like a sponge: the charge can sink in at different depths depending on how hard you squeeze (the voltage). The deeper the charge sits, the farther apart the "plates" effectively are, and the smaller the capacitance.

9.3 The three states (p-type substrate)

Put a voltage VG on the metal gate, with the semiconductor body grounded. Let Qm be the charge per unit area on the metal and Qs the charge per unit area in the semiconductor (Qm + Qs = 0 always). We take a p-type substrate, as your notes do.

Explain like I'm 10

The p-type body is a playground full of swings-with-kids (holes). Accumulation: the gate calls all kids to it; a crowd gathers at the fence. Depletion: the gate now pushes kids away; a kid-free zone appears near the fence, leaving only the chairs (fixed negative ions). Inversion: the push is so strong that the ground itself starts "making" a different type of kid (electrons) which gather at the fence. Now the surface behaves like an n-type material, even though the body is p-type. That thin layer of electrons is the channel of an nMOS transistor.

  • In depletion the charge is only the immobile ions; as VG rises the depletion width w grows.
  • Once w reaches its maximum wm, adding more gate voltage no longer widens the depletion region. Instead the extra charge shows up as mobile electrons in the inversion layer. The gate voltage at which this starts is the threshold voltage Vth.
  • For an n-type substrate everything mirrors: negative VG depletes, more negative inverts (holes form the channel), positive VG accumulates electrons.

9.4 Capacitance of the MOS structure

Two capacitors in series
Oxide capacitance (per unit area): Cox = [[ εox | tox ]] — independent of bias; tox = oxide thickness
Semiconductor / depletion capacitance: Cs = Cdep = [[ εs | w ]] — depends on w, hence on VG
[[1 | C]] = [[1 | Cox]] + [[1 | Cs]]
{{fig:mos_cv|Your page 3: the C–V curve. Normalised to C_ox.}}
  1. Accumulation (VG negative): holes sit right at the interface, so the semiconductor acts like a metal plate. Cdep is huge and C = Cox (C/Cox = 1).
  2. Depletion: as VG rises, w rises, Cdep = εs/w falls, so the series combination falls: C decreases.
  3. At threshold: w = wm, Cdep is at its minimum, and total C is at its minimum Cmin.
  4. Inversion: if the electron layer can follow the signal (low frequency or DC), the electrons act as the plate again and C climbs back up to Cox. At high frequency the electrons can't be generated fast enough, so C stays near Cmin. That is why your figure has several dashed curves.
Beyond your notes: real numbers for threshold
Fermi potential: φF = VT ln(NA/ni)    Max depletion width: wm = √( [[ 4 εs φF | q NA ]] )
Vth = VFB + 2φF + [[ √( 2 q εs NA (2φF) ) | Cox ]] (VFB = flat-band voltage from work-function differences and oxide charge)

Strong inversion starts when the surface band-bending equals 2φF, i.e. the surface is as n-type as the bulk is p-type.

Worked: p-type Si, NA = 1017 cm-3, ni = 1.5×1010, oxide tox = 2 nm (εox = 3.9ε0 = 3.45×10-13 F/cm). Find Cox, wm, Cmin/Cox, and Vth ignoring VFB.
  1. Cox = 3.45×10-13 / 2×10-7 cm = 1.73×10-6 F/cm² = 1.73 μF/cm² (17.3 fF/μm²).
  2. φF = 0.0259 × ln(1017/1.5×1010) = 0.0259 × 15.71 = 0.407 V.
  3. wm = √[4 × 1.04×10-12 × 0.407 / (1.6×10-19 × 1017)] = √(1.06×10-10) = 1.03×10-5 cm = 0.103 μm.
  4. Cdep,min = εs/wm = 1.04×10-12/1.03×10-5 = 1.01×10-7 F/cm². Series: Cmin = 1/(1/1.73×10-6 + 1/1.01×10-7) = 9.55×10-8 ⇒ Cmin/Cox ≈ 0.055.
  5. Depletion charge: Qd = q NA wm = 1.6×10-19 × 1017 × 1.03×10-5 = 1.65×10-7 C/cm². Vth (ignoring VFB) = 2φF + Qd/Cox = 0.814 + 0.095 = 0.91 V.

Reading: a thinner oxide ⇒ larger Cox ⇒ smaller second term in Vth and stronger control of the channel by the gate.

Exam traps
  • "Accumulation, depletion, inversion" order depends on the substrate polarity: for p-type it goes negative → positive VG. For n-type it is reversed.
  • Cox is fixed by tox; Cdep is the one that moves. In depletion they are in series, so the total is always smaller than Cox.
  • Larger oxide thickness ⇒ weaker vertical field E = V/tox ⇒ the gate has less control.

CHAPTER 10The MOSFET

A voltage-controlled switch that draws almost no current at its gate, is very fast, and burns very little power. Billions of them make a CPU.

Your second note-set, p.4 to p.8 Sections 10.7 to 10.10 go beyond your notes

10.1 What a MOSFET is

  • MOSFET = Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistor.
  • It can be used as a switch. Switching can be done at (i) very high speed and (ii) low power dissipation.
  • Four terminals: Source (S), Drain (D), Gate (G), Substrate / Bulk (B).
  • It is a unipolar device: nMOS current is due to electrons only; pMOS current is due to holes only.
Explain like I'm 10

A drawbridge. Source and drain are two banks of a river. When the gate voltage is low the bridge is up: no one crosses. When you raise the gate voltage the bridge comes down: cars (electrons) flow across. And the person cranking the bridge (the gate) never has to carry a car themselves, because they only use an electric field. That's why the gate takes almost no current.

Three things every engineer asks about a switch (your notes' list):

  1. At what VG does it turn ON? The threshold voltage Vth.
  2. RON: the ON resistance. Lower is better.
  3. Speed: the delay is τ = R C (resistance times the capacitance it must charge). Lower RON and smaller load C ⇒ faster.

10.2 Physical structure

{{fig:nmos|Your pages 4 to 5: nMOS cross-section. Left: the structure. Right: ON with an inversion channel.}}
  • nMOS: built on a p-type substrate. Two heavily doped n⁺ regions form the source and the drain. Between them, a thin SiO2 layer and the gate on top. A p⁺ contact (B) ties the body to a voltage.
  • pMOS: mirror image: an n-type substrate (or well), two p⁺ source/drain regions, an n⁺ body contact.
  • L = channel length (source to drain), W = channel width (into the page). The aspect ratio W/L is the main design knob.
  • Circuit symbols: nMOS has an arrow into the channel, pMOS has an arrow out (or a bubble on the gate in digital diagrams).

n-well CMOS process

{{fig:nwell|Your page 5: both transistors on one p-type wafer. The pMOS lives inside an n-well.}}

A single wafer can't be both p-type and n-type, so a lightly doped n-well is created inside the p-substrate. The nMOS is built directly in the p-substrate and the pMOS is built inside the n-well. That is the n-well process your notes name.

10.3 Types of MOSFET

(i) By charge carrier

nMOS (electrons) and pMOS (holes).

(ii) By electrical mode

Enhancement mode: normally OFF. No channel exists between source and drain at VGS = 0; you must create one. This is the type used in CMOS.

Depletion mode: normally ON. A channel already exists at VGS = 0; you apply voltage to remove it.

Combining both classifications gives four devices: nMOS enhancement, nMOS depletion, pMOS enhancement, pMOS depletion. (Your notes list four lines; the lines "pMOS enh, pMOS dep, nMOS enh" are followed by a last line that should read "nMOS dep".)

Why the body is tied that way

The source–body and drain–body p–n junctions must never be forward biased, otherwise current leaks into the substrate. So the p-substrate of an nMOS goes to the lowest voltage in the circuit, and the n-well of a pMOS goes to the highest. (Your note for pMOS shows source at "GND" in one place; in a CMOS circuit the pMOS source is tied to VDD, which is the reference that makes VGS and VDS negative relative to it. What matters is: VGS and VDS are negative.)

10.5 Operation of an nMOS

  1. Cut-off (OFF). VG = 0 V or VGS negative. There is no channel/path from source to drain for electrons. The source and drain are two n⁺ islands separated by p-type silicon (two back-to-back diodes), so no current can flow.
  2. Channel formation. Make VGS positive. As in the MOS capacitor (Chapter 9), the surface first depletes, and when VGS = VTH it inverts: a thin layer of electrons appears under the gate, joining source to drain. This is the onset of inversion. The channel is a resistor that the gate voltage controls.
  3. Vertical field: the gate field across the oxide is 𝓔 = V/tox. A thicker oxide lowers the field for the same V. The oxide capacitance Cox is fixed by tox, while Cdep depends on the state of the depletion region.
Quick check from your notes: "At what value of VG does the device turn ON?"

When the gate-to-source voltage reaches the threshold voltage: VGS ≥ VTH (for an nMOS enhancement device). Below it: cut-off. Typical VTH ≈ 0.3 to 0.7 V in modern CMOS.

10.6 The drain current: your derivation, step by step

Idea: current = charge per unit length × velocity

I = Q · v    Q = charge per unit length along the channel, v = velocity of carriers
Explain like I'm 10

Cars passing a checkpoint per second = (cars on each metre of road) × (how fast they're driving). Charge per length is the "cars per metre"; velocity is the driving speed.

  1. Inversion charge. The gate sets up a capacitor with capacitance Cox per unit area. The gate only pulls electrons into the channel once VGS exceeds Vth. The voltage that does the work is the overdrive voltage (VGS − VTH): the "extra voltage beyond VTH". With Q = C V, charge per unit length with channel width W:
    Q′ = W Cox (VGS − VTH)
  2. Effect of drain voltage. With the drain at VDS, the channel potential V(x) rises from 0 at the source (x = 0) to VDS at the drain (x = L). At position x the gate-to-channel voltage is reduced, so the total voltage experienced by the carriers is (VGS − Vth − V(x)):
    Q(x) = W Cox ( VGS − Vth − V(x) )
    Your notes tabulate it as VGS − Vth − 0, − 0.1, − 0.2, … − 1: the charge thins out towards the drain.
  3. Velocity. Drift: vd = μ𝓔 = − μ dV(x)/dx (the field is the slope of the potential).
  4. Current. Multiply charge × velocity (the sign is absorbed into the direction of I):
    I = W Cox ( VGS − Vth − V(x) ) · μ [[ dV(x) | dx ]]
  5. Integrate along the channel. The same current I flows at every x (steady state), so we can separate variables:
    ∫x=0L I dx = μ W Cox ∫V(x)=0VDS ( VGS − Vth − V(x) ) dV(x)
    Limits: at x = 0 the source is grounded, V(x) = 0; at x = L, V = VDS.
    I L = μ W Cox [ (VGS − Vth) VDS − VDS²/2 ]
  6. Result:
    Drain current, triode (linear) region
    ID = μn Cox ( [[W | L]] ) [ ( VGS − Vth ) VDS − [[ VDS² | 2 ]] ]
Careful

Your first boxed version of the result lacks the W/L factor; the final one includes it. Always include W/L. Units check: μ Cox has A/V², times V² gives A. ✔

Small VDS: the MOSFET as a resistor

For VDS ≪ VGS − Vth the VDS² term vanishes and ID ≈ μnCox(W/L)(VGS − Vth) VDS — linear in VDS. So the transistor behaves like a resistor whose value the gate controls:

RON = [[ VDS | ID ]] ≈ [[ 1 | μn Cox (W/L)(VGS − Vth) ]]

Wider transistor (W ↑), shorter channel (L ↓), higher mobility or bigger overdrive ⇒ smaller RON ⇒ faster (τ = RC).

10.7 What happens when VDS gets larger: pinch-off

Definition from your page 7
Your notes say

Pinch-off: thinning of the inversion charge due to V(x), and encroachment of the depletion layer.

{{fig:pinchoff|As V_DS grows, the gate-to-channel voltage at the drain end falls until the channel vanishes there.}}
Explain like I'm 10

Think of water flowing down a hose while someone squeezes the end. If you increase the water pressure difference between the two ends, the hose is squeezed most at the far end (where the gate has the weakest hold), until at the far end it's basically closed. But water still flows: it shoots through the last tiny gap at high speed, driven by the strong field there. The far-end gap barely changes, so the flow stops increasing even if you push harder. That's saturation.

  • At the drain end the gate-to-channel voltage is VGS − VDS. The channel vanishes there when this equals Vth, i.e. when VDS = VGS − Vth = Vov.
  • For VDS larger than that, the pinch-off point moves toward the source. The voltage across the remaining channel stays fixed at Vov, so the current stops growing.

Where the saturation formula comes from: put VDS = VGS − Vth into the triode formula: (Vov)(Vov) − Vov²/2 = Vov²/2. At this point the parabola of ID versus VDS reaches its peak (derivative zero), and beyond it the current is held flat at that peak value.

Channel-length modulation: in saturation the pinch-off point moves toward the source, so the effective length shrinks and the current rises slightly with VDS: ID = ½μnCox(W/L)Vov²(1 + λVDS). This is the MOSFET twin of the BJT Early effect (λ ≈ 1/VA).

Transconductance (gain of the transistor): gm = ∂ID/∂VGS = μnCox(W/L)Vov = 2ID/Vov.

Body effect: if the source is above the body potential by VSB, the threshold rises: Vth = Vth0 + γ(√(2φF + VSB) − √(2φF)).

Worked (beyond notes but classic): nMOS with μnCox = 100 μA/V², W/L = 10, Vth = 0.7 V. Find ID for (a) VGS = 1.7 V, VDS = 0.2 V; (b) VGS = 1.7 V, VDS = 2 V; (c) VGS = 0.5 V. Also the RON for small VDS.
  1. Vov = 1.7 − 0.7 = 1.0 V. k′(W/L) = 100 μA/V² × 10 = 1 mA/V².
  2. (a) VDS = 0.2 < Vov: triode. ID = 1 mA/V² × [1.0 × 0.2 − 0.2²/2] = 1 × (0.2 − 0.02) = 0.18 mA.
  3. (b) VDS = 2 ≥ Vov: saturation. ID = ½ × 1 mA/V² × 1.0² = 0.5 mA (independent of VDS).
  4. (c) VGS = 0.5 < Vth: cut-off, ID ≈ 0.
  5. RON ≈ 1/(1 mA/V² × 1.0 V) = 1 kΩ. With a 10 fF load, τ = RC = 1 kΩ × 10 fF = 10 ps.
Extra (delay): a nMOS switch with RON = 2 kΩ must charge a load of 50 fF. How long until it reaches 63 % of its final value? How does doubling W change it?

τ = R C = 2 kΩ × 50 fF = 100 ps (the 63 % time constant of an RC charge). Doubling W halves RON (R ∝ L/W), so τ = 50 ps if the load doesn't change. In real circuits wider transistors also add capacitance to their own drain and to the previous stage, so the gain is less than 2×.

10.8 Bringing it together: CMOS and the inverter

{{fig:inverter|A CMOS inverter: one pMOS pulling up, one nMOS pulling down.}}
Explain like I'm 10

Two doors on a room, wired so exactly one is open at any time: one door leads to the "1" (power) hallway, the other to the "0" (ground) hallway. When the input is high (1), the bottom door opens and the output is connected to 0. When the input is low (0), the top door opens and the output is connected to 1. Since one door is always shut, there is never a straight path from power to ground, so almost no current is wasted while the input isn't changing. That's why CMOS uses so little power.

  • Vin = 0: nMOS off (VGS = 0 < Vth), pMOS on ⇒ Vout = VDD (logic 1).
  • Vin = VDD: nMOS on, pMOS off ⇒ Vout = 0 (logic 0).
  • Static power ≈ 0 (only tiny leakage). Dynamic power ≈ C VDD² f, only when switching.
  • Since μp ≈ μn/2 to /3, the pMOS is made 2 to 3 times wider to balance rise and fall times.

10.9 Why this course is called VLSI

One inverter needs 2 transistors, a NAND gate 4, a flip-flop about 20 to 30, a 64-bit adder thousands. Reaching millions to billions of transistors is only possible because the MOSFET is (i) tiny, (ii) needs no DC gate current, (iii) dissipates almost no static power in CMOS, (iv) can be built by the same lithography process on the same wafer. Scaling W, L and tox down together (Moore's law) is how speed rose and cost per transistor fell for 50 years.

10.10 Short-channel effects (for awareness)

  • Velocity saturation (your Chapter 6): at high lateral field carriers reach vsat, so ID ∝ Vov (linear) instead of Vov².
  • Drain-induced barrier lowering (DIBL) and punch-through: a high drain voltage reduces the barrier near the source in very short channels.
  • Subthreshold leakage: below Vth the current does not drop to zero but falls exponentially, about 60–100 mV per decade.
  • Gate-oxide leakage: as tox shrinks below 2 nm, electrons tunnel through, motivating high-k dielectrics.
Exam traps for the MOSFET
  • nMOS turns on with a high gate; pMOS turns on with a low gate. Never mix.
  • Threshold voltage is where inversion begins; the channel doesn't appear at any VGS below it.
  • The linear formula holds only while VDS < VGS − Vth.
  • μn is not a constant; in the channel it is lower than in bulk silicon because of surface scattering, and it falls at high gate voltage.
  • "Depletion-mode" is not the same as the "depletion region". The mode name refers to a device with a channel present at zero gate bias.
