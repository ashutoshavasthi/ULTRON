CHAPTER 12Practice Bank with Full Solutions

Try each question on paper first, then open the answer. Ordered like the course. The last group tests understanding in words.

How to use it

Do part A to F once, then redo only the ones you missed two days later. When a question looks new, it is almost always one of your class questions with different numbers.

A. Bands and Fermi statistics

A1. Compute the thermal energy kBT at 400 K in eV and meV.

kBT = 8.617×10-5 × 400 = 0.03447 eV = 34.5 meV. (Scales linearly with T: 300 → 25.85, 400 → 34.5.)

A2. For N carbon atoms, state the number of outer states and electrons, and how many states are in the valence band and conduction band.

Outer states 8N (2N from 2s, 6N from 2p). Outer electrons 4N. After band formation: VB = 4N states (all filled with 4N electrons), CB = 4N states (empty at 0 K).

A3. Two semiconductors have E–k curves near the band edge: A with d²E/dk² = 2×10-38 J·m², B with 0.5×10-38. Which has higher mobility (same τ)? Find m*A/m*B.

m* = ħ²/(d²E/dk²), so m* is inversely proportional to the curvature. m*A/m*B = 0.5/2 = 0.25. A is lighter, so with equal τ, μ = qτ/m* is 4× higher for A, and A has the higher current density.

A4. At 300 K, what is the probability that a state 0.05 eV above EF is occupied? What about 0.05 eV below?

Above: f = 1/(1 + e0.05/0.02585) = 1/(1 + 6.92) = 0.126. Below: 1 − 0.126 = 0.874. (Symmetry: f(EF + Δ) + f(EF − Δ) = 1.)

A5. Explain why GaAs is used for LEDs but Si is not.

GaAs is a direct-gap semiconductor: an electron and hole at the same k can recombine and release a photon directly. Si is indirect: recombination also needs a phonon to conserve momentum, which is a rare three-body event, so energy mostly goes to heat.

B. Carrier concentration

B1. Si (ni = 1.5×1010) is doped with ND = 2×1015 cm-3. Find n and p. What type is it?

n ≈ ND = 2×1015; p = ni²/n = 2.25×1020/2×1015 = 1.125×105 cm-3. n ≫ p ⇒ n-type.

B2. p-type Si with NA = 5×1016. Find p, n and the position of EF relative to Ei.

p ≈ 5×1016; n = 2.25×1020/5×1016 = 4500 cm-3. EF − Ei = −kBT ln(p/ni) = −0.0259 × ln(3.33×106) = −0.389 eV: 0.39 eV below mid-gap, i.e. only about 0.17 eV above Ev for a 1.12 eV gap.

B3. A Si sample has ND = 8×1015 and NA = 3×1015. Which type is it and what are n, p?

Net ND − NA = 5×1015 ≫ ni, so n-type, n ≈ 5×1015, p = 2.25×1020/5×1015 = 4.5×104.

B4. Why does n·p stay equal to ni² even after heavy doping, and what happens to the minority-carrier count?

Generation (thermal) and recombination (electron meets hole) reach a balance where the rates are proportional to n·p. Extra electrons from donors increase the recombination rate of holes, so holes fall until np returns to the equilibrium value ni². Minority carriers decrease by the same factor by which majority carriers increase.

C. Transport

C1. n-type Si with ND = 1015 cm-3, μn = 1350. Find σ and ρ. Then find the drift velocity and current density for 𝓔 = 100 V/cm.

σ = qNDμn = 1.6×10-19 × 1015 × 1350 = 0.216 S/cm, ρ = 4.63 Ω·cm. Vd = 1350 × 100 = 1.35×105 cm/s (still ≪ thermal speed ≈ 107 cm/s). J = σ𝓔 = 0.216 × 100 = 21.6 A/cm².

C2. Find the mean free time τ if μn = 1350 cm²/V·s and m* = 0.26 m0.

μ = 0.135 m²/V·s in SI. τ = μ m*/q = 0.135 × 0.26 × 9.11×10-31 / 1.6×10-19 = 2.0×10-13 s = 0.2 ps. With a thermal speed of 107 cm/s, the mean free path is about 20 nm.

C3. Using Einstein's relation find Dp for μp = 450 at 300 K, and Lp if τp = 1 μs.

Dp = μpVT = 450 × 0.0259 = 11.7 cm²/s. Lp = √(Dpτp) = √(11.7 × 10-6) = 3.4×10-3 cm = 34 μm.

C4. An electron density in Si varies linearly from 1016 cm-3 at x = 0 to 1015 cm-3 at x = 1 μm. With Dn = 30 cm²/s find the diffusion current density and its direction.

dn/dx = (1015 − 1016)/10-4 cm = −9×1019 cm-4. Jn = qDndn/dx = 1.6×10-19 × 30 × (−9×1019) = −432 A/cm². Electrons diffuse to +x (down the gradient), so the current flows toward −x.

C5. Light generates carriers in n-type Si (ND = 1015) at G = 1019 cm-3s-1. If the minority lifetime is 1 μs, find the steady-state excess carriers and the change in minority concentration.

In steady state generation = recombination: G = U = Δp/τ ⇒ Δp = Gτ = 1019 × 10-6 = 1013 cm-3. Equilibrium minority p0 = 2.25×105, so p ≈ 1013, an increase of about 4×107 times. The majority electrons change by only 1013/1015 = 1 %. This is why photodetectors respond to minority carriers.

C6. Sketch and explain why mobility peaks at an intermediate temperature in a moderately doped Si sample.

Low T: slow carriers are strongly scattered by ionised impurities, μII is small and rising with T. High T: lattice vibrations (phonons) scatter more, μph falls with T. Since 1/μ = 1/μph + 1/μII, the smaller one dominates at each end, giving a hill with a maximum where the two are comparable. More doping raises the low-T side (more impurity scattering) and lowers the peak.

D. p–n junction

D1. Find V0, W, xn, xp for NA = ND = 1016 cm-3, ni = 1.5×1010, T = 300 K.

V0 = 0.0259 ln(1032/2.25×1020) = 0.0259 × 26.82 = 0.695 V. W = √[(2 × 1.04×10-12/1.6×10-19)(2×10-16)(0.695)] = √(1.81×10-9) = 4.25×10-5 cm = 0.425 μm. Symmetric doping ⇒ xn = xp = 0.212 μm.

D2. In a junction with NA = 4×1016 and ND = 1016, which side has the wider depletion layer, and by what factor?

xn/xp = NA/ND = 4. The n-side (lightly doped) has the wider layer, four times the p-side layer. With W = 1 μm, xn = 0.8 μm and xp = 0.2 μm.

D3. A silicon diode has V0 = 0.7 V and W = 0.4 μm at zero bias. What is W at VR = 4.3 V?

W ∝ √(V0 + VR). W = 0.4 × √(5.0/0.7) = 0.4 × 2.673 = 1.07 μm.

D4. A diode carries 1 mA at 0.65 V. What voltage is needed for 10 mA? For 100 μA? (T = 300 K)

I ∝ eV/VT, so ΔV = VT ln(I2/I1). ×10 current: ΔV = 0.0259 × 2.303 = 59.6 mV. So 10 mA at 0.710 V, and 100 μA at 0.590 V (about 60 mV per decade).

D5. Why does an ideal reverse-biased diode carry −Is regardless of the voltage, and why does a real one show a slow rise before breakdown?

In reverse bias the barrier is so high that no majority carriers cross; the only current is the drift of the few thermally generated minority carriers near the junction, whose supply is fixed by temperature (not by voltage), so it saturates at Is. In real diodes, thermal generation inside the growing depletion region adds a component that increases with W (and therefore with VR), plus surface leakage.

D6. Classify the breakdown type: (a) VBR = 3.3 V heavily doped junction; (b) VBR = 40 V lightly doped junction.

(a) Zener (tunnelling; high doping ⇒ very thin depletion region ⇒ huge field at low voltage; VBR < 5 V). (b) Avalanche (impact ionisation; wide, lightly doped region ⇒ carriers accelerate over long distance; VBR > 8 V).

D7. For the diode of Section 7.9, what is the hole-injection fraction if ND is raised to 1018 and NA lowered to 1016?

The ratio Ip/In = (Dp/Dn)(Ln/Lp)(NA/ND) = (10/18)(10/5)(1016/1018) = 0.556 × 2 × 0.01 = 0.0111. So the electron current now dominates: hole fraction = 0.0111/1.0111 = 1.1 %. The majority carrier of the heavily-doped side always carries most of the current.

E. BJT

E1. An npn BJT has IE = 5 mA and α = 0.99. Find IC, IB, β.

IC = αIE = 4.95 mA. IB = IE − IC = 0.05 mA. β = 4.95/0.05 = 99 (α/(1−α) = 0.99/0.01 ✔).

E2. A BJT with β = 150 has IB = 50 μA. Find IC, IE, α.

IC = 150 × 50 μA = 7.5 mA; IE = 7.55 mA; α = 150/151 = 0.9934.

E3. B = 0.99 and γE = 0.98. Find α and β. Which one would you improve first to increase β, and why?

α = 0.99 × 0.98 = 0.9702; β = 0.9702/0.0298 = 32.6. Either factor matters equally at first order; but the emitter injection efficiency is usually easiest to fix (increase the emitter/base doping ratio), while B improves with a thinner base. Making γE = 0.995 would give α = 0.985 and β ≈ 66: doubling the gain.

E4. State the bias on EBJ and CBJ for (a) an amplifier, (b) a closed switch, (c) an open switch.

(a) Active: EBJ forward, CBJ reverse. (b) Saturation: both forward. (c) Cut-off: both reverse.

E5. A BJT has VA = 80 V and IC = 2 mA. Find the output resistance and the change in IC if VCE increases by 8 V.

ro ≈ VA/IC = 80/2 mA = 40 kΩ. ΔIC = ΔVCE/ro = 8/40 kΩ = 0.2 mA (a 10 % increase).

E6. Why must the base of a BJT be thin and lightly doped while the emitter is thick and heavily doped?

Thin base: injected electrons cross before recombining ⇒ base transport factor B → 1 and small base current. Light base doping: few holes to recombine with ⇒ smaller IB,rec, and fewer holes to be injected back into the emitter. Heavy emitter doping: the electron injection current from the emitter far exceeds the hole current back into it ⇒ γE → 1. Together α → 1 and β is large.

F. MOS and MOSFET

F1. Find Cox for tox = 5 nm SiO2. What happens to Cox and the field if tox is halved at the same VG?

Cox = 3.45×10-13/5×10-7 = 6.9×10-7 F/cm² (0.69 μF/cm²). Halving tox doubles Cox and doubles the oxide field V/tox. Higher Cox ⇒ more inversion charge and more current for the same overdrive; the price is oxide reliability and tunnelling leakage.

F2. For an n-type MOS capacitor (n-substrate), name the state at (a) large positive VG, (b) small negative VG, (c) large negative VG.

(a) Accumulation (electrons attracted to the interface). (b) Depletion (electrons repelled, uncovered donor ions +). (c) Inversion (holes collect at the surface, forming a p-type channel: the pMOS channel).

F3. nMOS: μnCox = 200 μA/V², W/L = 5, Vth = 0.5 V. Find ID at VGS = 1.5 V for VDS = 0.4 V, 0.8 V, 3 V. Also gm in saturation.

k′W/L = 1 mA/V²; Vov = 1.0 V. VDS = 0.4: triode: 1 × (1×0.4 − 0.08) = 0.32 mA. VDS = 0.8: triode: 1 × (0.8 − 0.32) = 0.48 mA. VDS = 3: saturation (3 ≥ 1): ½ × 1 × 1² = 0.5 mA. gm = k′(W/L)Vov = 1 mS. Notice the current rises quickly then flattens at 0.5 mA, the peak of the triode parabola at VDS = Vov.

F4. Derive, in five lines, the triode current of an nMOS.
  1. Q(x) = W Cox(VGS − Vth − V(x)).
  2. v = μ𝓔 = μ dV/dx (magnitude).
  3. I = Q v = W Cox(VGS − Vth − V) μ dV/dx.
  4. Integrate ∫0L I dx = μ W Cox ∫0VDS (VGS − Vth − V) dV.
  5. I L = μ W Cox[(VGS − Vth)VDS − VDS²/2] ⇒ ID = μnCox(W/L)[…].
F5. Why do pMOS transistors in a CMOS inverter have W about 2 to 3 times larger than the nMOS?

Holes have a lower mobility (μp ≈ μn/2 to /3) so for equal W/L the pMOS conducts less current and charges the load slower. Making Wp ≈ 2 to 3 × Wn equalises the drive currents and gives equal rise and fall times.

F6. An nMOS switch (RON = 1 kΩ) drives a 20 fF load. What is τ? What limits how small you can make τ?

τ = RC = 1 kΩ × 20 fF = 20 ps. Increasing W lowers RON but also raises the transistor's own parasitic capacitance and the load presented to the previous stage. Velocity saturation and interconnect resistance also set limits.

G. Explain-in-words questions (viva / short-answer)

G1. Why is the Fermi level called an "imaginary construct"?

Because it is just the energy at which f(E) = ½. There need not be any allowed state at that energy; in a semiconductor EF usually sits inside the forbidden gap.

G2. Why is there no net current at thermal equilibrium even though carriers keep diffusing and drifting?

At every point the diffusion current is exactly cancelled by an equal and opposite drift current (in the junction: Idiffusion = Idrift = Is). This is equivalent to EF being flat throughout the device.

G3. What two things must be true for a hole to be a useful concept?

The valence band must be nearly full (so tracking the few empty states is simpler), and the empty state must respond to fields like a particle with positive charge and positive effective mass. Both hold in a semiconductor.

G4. Why does the depletion region penetrate more into the lightly doped side?

The uncovered charge on both sides must be equal: q ND xn = q NA xp. Fewer ions per unit volume on the light side, so a larger thickness is needed to hold the same total charge.

G5. Why does a MOSFET need almost no gate current?

The gate is separated from the channel by an insulating oxide, forming a capacitor. Only charging current flows while the gate voltage changes; in DC the oxide blocks current. Field effect: the field, not a current, controls the channel.

13.1 The 15 things most likely to lose marks

  1. Band gaps: Ge 0.7, Si 1.1, GaAs 1.34, GaN 3.4 eV. Si and Ge are indirect; GaAs and GaN are direct.
  2. kBT = 25.87 meV at 300 K, VT = 25.9 mV. Use eV vs J consistently.
  3. At T = 0 K: f = 1 below EF, ½ at EF, 0 above.
  4. m* ∝ 1/curvature; more curvature = lighter = more mobile = more current.
  5. μn > μp because mh* > me*.
  6. Mobility vs T is a hill: impurity scattering at low T, phonons at high T; Matthiessen's rule adds reciprocals.
  7. Diffusion: Jn = +qDndn/dx, Jp = −qDpdp/dx. Einstein: D = μVT.
  8. np = ni² only at equilibrium; U = 0 there.
  9. Depletion region: xn/xp = NA/ND; more into the lighter side. Bias enters as (V0 ± V).
  10. Flat EF = no current; sloped bands = field.
  11. Is has D/(L N) with the opposite side's doping; smaller ni (larger Eg) ⇒ smaller Is ⇒ larger forward voltage (Si > Ge).
  12. Zener < 5 V (tunnelling, heavy doping); avalanche > 8 V (impact ionisation, light doping).
  13. BJT: active = F/R; α = Bγ; β = α/(1−α); thin light base, heavy emitter; Early voltage from base-width modulation.
  14. MOS capacitor (p-substrate): negative VG accumulation, small + depletion, large + inversion. 1/C = 1/Cox + 1/Cdep; Cmin at w = wm.
  15. MOSFET triode current has W/L and the VDS²/2 term; saturation begins at VDS = VGS − Vth.

Final pep talk, like I'm 10

Every device in this course is just one idea repeated: carriers are pushed (drift) or spread (diffusion), and something blocks or opens their path. In the diode the blocker is the depletion region; in the BJT it is a thin base; in the MOSFET it is a gate-controlled channel. If you can draw the band picture and say who is pushing whom, you can answer nearly any question.
