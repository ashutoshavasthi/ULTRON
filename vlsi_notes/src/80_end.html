How to use it

Do parts A to M once, then redo only the ones you missed two days later. When a question looks new, it is almost always one of your class questions with different numbers.

A. Bands and Fermi statistics

A1. Compute the thermal energy kBT at 400 K in eV and meV.

kBT = 8.617×10-5 × 400 = 0.03447 eV = 34.5 meV. (Scales linearly with T: 300 → 25.85, 400 → 34.5.)

A2. For N carbon atoms, state the number of outer states and electrons, and how many states are in the valence band and conduction band.

Outer states 8N (2N from 2s, 6N from 2p). Outer electrons 4N. After band formation: VB = 4N states (all filled with 4N electrons), CB = 4N states (empty at 0 K).

A3. Two semiconductors have E–k curves near the band edge: A with d²E/dk² = 2×10-38 J·m², B with 0.5×10-38. Which has higher mobility (same τ)? Find m*A/m*B.

m* = ħ²/(d²E/dk²), so m* is inversely proportional to the curvature. m*A/m*B = 0.5/2 = 0.25. A is lighter, so with equal τ, μ = qτ/m* is 4× higher for A, and A has the higher current density.

A4. At 300 K, what is the probability that a state 0.05 eV above EF is occupied? What about 0.05 eV below?

Above: f = 1/(1 + e0.05/0.02585) = 1/(1 + 6.92) = 0.126. Below: 1 − 0.126 = 0.874. (Symmetry: f(EF + Δ) + f(EF − Δ) = 1.)

A5. Explain why GaAs is used for LEDs but Si is not.

GaAs is a direct-gap semiconductor: an electron and hole at the same k can recombine and release a photon directly. Si is indirect: recombination also needs a phonon to conserve momentum, which is a rare three-body event, so energy mostly goes to heat.

B. Carrier concentration

B1. Si (ni = 1.5×1010) is doped with ND = 2×1015 cm-3. Find n and p. What type is it?

n ≈ ND = 2×1015; p = ni²/n = 2.25×1020/2×1015 = 1.125×105 cm-3. n ≫ p ⇒ n-type.

B2. p-type Si with NA = 5×1016. Find p, n and the position of EF relative to Ei.

p ≈ 5×1016; n = 2.25×1020/5×1016 = 4500 cm-3. EF − Ei = −kBT ln(p/ni) = −0.0259 × ln(3.33×106) = −0.389 eV: 0.39 eV below mid-gap, i.e. only about 0.17 eV above Ev for a 1.12 eV gap.

B3. A Si sample has ND = 8×1015 and NA = 3×1015. Which type is it and what are n, p?

Net ND − NA = 5×1015 ≫ ni, so n-type, n ≈ 5×1015, p = 2.25×1020/5×1015 = 4.5×104.

B4. Why does n·p stay equal to ni² even after heavy doping, and what happens to the minority-carrier count?

Generation (thermal) and recombination (electron meets hole) reach a balance where the rates are proportional to n·p. Extra electrons from donors increase the recombination rate of holes, so holes fall until np returns to the equilibrium value ni². Minority carriers decrease by the same factor by which majority carriers increase.

C. Transport

C1. n-type Si with ND = 1015 cm-3, μn = 1350. Find σ and ρ. Then find the drift velocity and current density for 𝓔 = 100 V/cm.

σ = qNDμn = 1.6×10-19 × 1015 × 1350 = 0.216 S/cm, ρ = 4.63 Ω·cm. Vd = 1350 × 100 = 1.35×105 cm/s (still ≪ thermal speed ≈ 107 cm/s). J = σ𝓔 = 0.216 × 100 = 21.6 A/cm².

C2. Find the mean free time τ if μn = 1350 cm²/V·s and m* = 0.26 m0.

μ = 0.135 m²/V·s in SI. τ = μ m*/q = 0.135 × 0.26 × 9.11×10-31 / 1.6×10-19 = 2.0×10-13 s = 0.2 ps. With a thermal speed of 107 cm/s, the mean free path is about 20 nm.

C3. Using Einstein's relation find Dp for μp = 450 at 300 K, and Lp if τp = 1 μs.

Dp = μpVT = 450 × 0.0259 = 11.7 cm²/s. Lp = √(Dpτp) = √(11.7 × 10-6) = 3.4×10-3 cm = 34 μm.

C4. An electron density in Si varies linearly from 1016 cm-3 at x = 0 to 1015 cm-3 at x = 1 μm. With Dn = 30 cm²/s find the diffusion current density and its direction.

dn/dx = (1015 − 1016)/10-4 cm = −9×1019 cm-4. Jn = qDndn/dx = 1.6×10-19 × 30 × (−9×1019) = −432 A/cm². Electrons diffuse to +x (down the gradient), so the current flows toward −x.

C5. Light generates carriers in n-type Si (ND = 1015) at G = 1019 cm-3s-1. If the minority lifetime is 1 μs, find the steady-state excess carriers and the change in minority concentration.

In steady state generation = recombination: G = U = Δp/τ ⇒ Δp = Gτ = 1019 × 10-6 = 1013 cm-3. Equilibrium minority p0 = 2.25×105, so p ≈ 1013, an increase of about 4×107 times. The majority electrons change by only 1013/1015 = 1 %. This is why photodetectors respond to minority carriers.

C6. Sketch and explain why mobility peaks at an intermediate temperature in a moderately doped Si sample.

Low T: slow carriers are strongly scattered by ionised impurities, μII is small and rising with T. High T: lattice vibrations (phonons) scatter more, μph falls with T. Since 1/μ = 1/μph + 1/μII, the smaller one dominates at each end, giving a hill with a maximum where the two are comparable. More doping raises the low-T side (more impurity scattering) and lowers the peak.

D. p–n junction

D1. Find V0, W, xn, xp for NA = ND = 1016 cm-3, ni = 1.5×1010, T = 300 K.

V0 = 0.0259 ln(1032/2.25×1020) = 0.0259 × 26.82 = 0.695 V. W = √[(2 × 1.04×10-12/1.6×10-19)(2×10-16)(0.695)] = √(1.81×10-9) = 4.25×10-5 cm = 0.425 μm. Symmetric doping ⇒ xn = xp = 0.212 μm.

D2. In a junction with NA = 4×1016 and ND = 1016, which side has the wider depletion layer, and by what factor?

xn/xp = NA/ND = 4. The n-side (lightly doped) has the wider layer, four times the p-side layer. With W = 1 μm, xn = 0.8 μm and xp = 0.2 μm.

D3. A silicon diode has V0 = 0.7 V and W = 0.4 μm at zero bias. What is W at VR = 4.3 V?

W ∝ √(V0 + VR). W = 0.4 × √(5.0/0.7) = 0.4 × 2.673 = 1.07 μm.

D4. A diode carries 1 mA at 0.65 V. What voltage is needed for 10 mA? For 100 μA? (T = 300 K)

I ∝ eV/VT, so ΔV = VT ln(I2/I1). ×10 current: ΔV = 0.0259 × 2.303 = 59.6 mV. So 10 mA at 0.710 V, and 100 μA at 0.590 V (about 60 mV per decade).

D5. Why does an ideal reverse-biased diode carry −Is regardless of the voltage, and why does a real one show a slow rise before breakdown?

In reverse bias the barrier is so high that no majority carriers cross; the only current is the drift of the few thermally generated minority carriers near the junction, whose supply is fixed by temperature (not by voltage), so it saturates at Is. In real diodes, thermal generation inside the growing depletion region adds a component that increases with W (and therefore with VR), plus surface leakage.

D6. Classify the breakdown type: (a) VBR = 3.3 V heavily doped junction; (b) VBR = 40 V lightly doped junction.

(a) Zener (tunnelling; high doping ⇒ very thin depletion region ⇒ huge field at low voltage; VBR < 5 V). (b) Avalanche (impact ionisation; wide, lightly doped region ⇒ carriers accelerate over long distance; VBR > 8 V).

D7. For the diode of Section 7.9, what is the hole-injection fraction if ND is raised to 1018 and NA lowered to 1016?

The ratio Ip/In = (Dp/Dn)(Ln/Lp)(NA/ND) = (10/18)(10/5)(1016/1018) = 0.556 × 2 × 0.01 = 0.0111. So the electron current now dominates: hole fraction = 0.0111/1.0111 = 1.1 %. The majority carrier of the heavily-doped side always carries most of the current.

E. BJT

E1. An npn BJT has IE = 5 mA and α = 0.99. Find IC, IB, β.

IC = αIE = 4.95 mA. IB = IE − IC = 0.05 mA. β = 4.95/0.05 = 99 (α/(1−α) = 0.99/0.01 ✔).

E2. A BJT with β = 150 has IB = 50 μA. Find IC, IE, α.

IC = 150 × 50 μA = 7.5 mA; IE = 7.55 mA; α = 150/151 = 0.9934.

E3. B = 0.99 and γE = 0.98. Find α and β. Which one would you improve first to increase β, and why?

α = 0.99 × 0.98 = 0.9702; β = 0.9702/0.0298 = 32.6. Either factor matters equally at first order; but the emitter injection efficiency is usually easiest to fix (increase the emitter/base doping ratio), while B improves with a thinner base. Making γE = 0.995 would give α = 0.985 and β ≈ 66: doubling the gain.

E4. State the bias on EBJ and CBJ for (a) an amplifier, (b) a closed switch, (c) an open switch.

(a) Active: EBJ forward, CBJ reverse. (b) Saturation: both forward. (c) Cut-off: both reverse.

E5. A BJT has VA = 80 V and IC = 2 mA. Find the output resistance and the change in IC if VCE increases by 8 V.

ro ≈ VA/IC = 80/2 mA = 40 kΩ. ΔIC = ΔVCE/ro = 8/40 kΩ = 0.2 mA (a 10 % increase).

E6. Why must the base of a BJT be thin and lightly doped while the emitter is thick and heavily doped?

Thin base: injected electrons cross before recombining ⇒ base transport factor B → 1 and small base current. Light base doping: few holes to recombine with ⇒ smaller IB,rec, and fewer holes to be injected back into the emitter. Heavy emitter doping: the electron injection current from the emitter far exceeds the hole current back into it ⇒ γE → 1. Together α → 1 and β is large.

F. MOS and MOSFET

F1. Find Cox for tox = 5 nm SiO2. What happens to Cox and the field if tox is halved at the same VG?

Cox = 3.45×10-13/5×10-7 = 6.9×10-7 F/cm² (0.69 μF/cm²). Halving tox doubles Cox and doubles the oxide field V/tox. Higher Cox ⇒ more inversion charge and more current for the same overdrive; the price is oxide reliability and tunnelling leakage.

F2. For an n-type MOS capacitor (n-substrate), name the state at (a) large positive VG, (b) small negative VG, (c) large negative VG.

(a) Accumulation (electrons attracted to the interface). (b) Depletion (electrons repelled, uncovered donor ions +). (c) Inversion (holes collect at the surface, forming a p-type channel: the pMOS channel).

F3. nMOS: μnCox = 200 μA/V², W/L = 5, Vth = 0.5 V. Find ID at VGS = 1.5 V for VDS = 0.4 V, 0.8 V, 3 V. Also gm in saturation.

k′W/L = 1 mA/V²; Vov = 1.0 V. VDS = 0.4: triode: 1 × (1×0.4 − 0.08) = 0.32 mA. VDS = 0.8: triode: 1 × (0.8 − 0.32) = 0.48 mA. VDS = 3: saturation (3 ≥ 1): ½ × 1 × 1² = 0.5 mA. gm = k′(W/L)Vov = 1 mS. Notice the current rises quickly then flattens at 0.5 mA, the peak of the triode parabola at VDS = Vov.

F4. Derive, in five lines, the triode current of an nMOS.
  1. Q(x) = W Cox(VGS − Vth − V(x)).
  2. v = μ𝓔 = μ dV/dx (magnitude).
  3. I = Q v = W Cox(VGS − Vth − V) μ dV/dx.
  4. Integrate ∫0L I dx = μ W Cox ∫0VDS (VGS − Vth − V) dV.
  5. I L = μ W Cox[(VGS − Vth)VDS − VDS²/2] ⇒ ID = μnCox(W/L)[…].
F5. Why do pMOS transistors in a CMOS inverter have W about 2 to 3 times larger than the nMOS?

Holes have a lower mobility (μp ≈ μn/2 to /3) so for equal W/L the pMOS conducts less current and charges the load slower. Making Wp ≈ 2 to 3 × Wn equalises the drive currents and gives equal rise and fall times.

F6. An nMOS switch (RON = 1 kΩ) drives a 20 fF load. What is τ? What limits how small you can make τ?

τ = RC = 1 kΩ × 20 fF = 20 ps. Increasing W lowers RON but also raises the transistor's own parasitic capacitance and the load presented to the previous stage. Velocity saturation and interconnect resistance also set limits.

H. The MOSFET in depth (Chapter 11)

H1. nMOS with μnCox(W/L) = 2 mA/V², Vth = 0.6 V, VGS = 1.4 V. Find the region and ID for VDS = 0.3 V and 1.5 V.

Vov = 0.8 V. VDS = 0.3 < 0.8 ⇒ triode: 2 × (0.8 × 0.3 − 0.045) = 0.39 mA. VDS = 1.5 ≥ 0.8 ⇒ saturation: ½ × 2 × 0.64 = 0.64 mA.

H2. pMOS: μpCox = 60 μA/V², W/L = 20, Vtp = −0.5 V. Source at 1.8 V, gate at 0.6 V, drain at 0.9 V. Region and current?

Use magnitudes: |VGS| = 1.2 V, overdrive 1.2 − 0.5 = 0.7 V, |VDS| = 0.9 V ≥ 0.7 ⇒ saturation. kp = 60 × 20 = 1.2 mA/V². ISD = ½ × 1.2 × 0.49 = 0.294 mA, flowing from source to drain.

H3. An nMOS (k = 0.4 mA/V², Vth = 0.5 V, VGS = 1.5 V) has RD from VDD = 3 V to the drain. Find ID and VDS for RD = 5 kΩ and 20 kΩ.

5 kΩ: assume saturation: ID = ½ × 0.4 × 1² = 0.2 mA; VDS = 3 − 0.2 × 5 = 2 V ≥ 1 V ✓. ID = 0.2 mA, VDS = 2 V.

20 kΩ: saturation would give VDS = 3 − 4 = −1 V: impossible, so the device is in triode. Solve 0.4(VDS − VDS²/2) = (3 − VDS)/20 ⇒ 4VDS² − 9VDS + 3 = 0 ⇒ VDS = (9 − √33)/8 = 0.41 V, ID = (3 − 0.41)/20 = 0.13 mA. Check 0.41 < 1 ✓.

H4. A process has n = 1.5. Find the sub-threshold slope. With Vth = 0.45 V, by how many decades does the current drop from VGS = Vth to VGS = 0?

S = 1.5 × 0.0259 × ln10 = 89 mV/decade. 450/89 = 5 decades (a factor of 105).

H5. In saturation an nMOS carries 1.0 mA at VDS = 1 V and 1.1 mA at VDS = 3 V. Find λ, VA and ro.

1.1/1.0 = (1 + 3λ)/(1 + λ) ⇒ 1.1 + 1.1λ = 1 + 3λ ⇒ λ = 0.053 V-1, VA = 19 V. ro = ΔV/ΔI = 2 V/0.1 mA = 20 kΩ.

H6. Vth0 = 0.4 V, γ = 0.5 V½, 2φF = 0.8 V. What VSB raises Vth to 0.6 V?

0.2 = 0.5(√(0.8 + VSB) − 0.894) ⇒ √(0.8 + VSB) = 1.294 ⇒ VSB = 0.88 V.

H7. A process is scaled by S = 1.4 (one "node") with constant-field scaling. What happens to area, delay, power per gate and power density? What if voltage is held constant instead?

Constant field: area × 1/1.96 = 0.51, delay × 0.71, power × 0.51, power density × 1. Constant voltage: delay × 1/1.96 = 0.51, power × 1.4, power density × 1.4³ = 2.7, fields × 1.4.

H8. With Ec = 4 V/μm, compare the velocity-saturation factor 1/(1 + VDS/EcL) at VDS = 1 V for L = 2 μm and L = 50 nm. Why does the short device's current become linear in Vov?

L = 2 μm: EcL = 8 V ⇒ factor 1/1.125 = 0.89. L = 50 nm: EcL = 0.2 V ⇒ 1/6 = 0.17. Carriers move at vsat regardless of the field, so I = (charge per length) × vsat = WCoxVovvsat: linear in Vov.

I. CMOS inverter and gates (Chapter 12)

I1. VDD = 3.3 V, Vtn = |Vtp| = 0.5 V. Find VM when kn = kp and when kn = 4kp.

kn = kp: VM = 1.65 V (= VDD/2). kn = 4kp: r = 0.5 ⇒ VM = (0.5 + 0.5 × 2.8)/1.5 = 1.27 V; the stronger nMOS pulls the threshold down.

I2. Matched inverter, VDD = 3.3 V, Vt = 0.6 V. Find VIL, VIH, NML, NMH.

VIL = (9.9 + 1.2)/8 = 1.39 V; VIH = (16.5 − 1.2)/8 = 1.91 V; NML = 1.39 − 0 = 1.39 V; NMH = 3.3 − 1.91 = 1.39 V.

I3. Rn = 8 kΩ, Rp = 20 kΩ, CL = 15 fF. Find tpHL, tpLH, tp, and the pMOS width factor that balances them.

tpHL = 0.69 × 8k × 15f = 83 ps; tpLH = 0.69 × 20k × 15f = 207 ps; tp = 145 ps. Widen the pMOS by 20/8 = 2.5×.

I4. A chip has 107 gates, each driving 2 fF, VDD = 1 V, clock 2 GHz, activity 0.1. Dynamic power?

P = 0.1 × 107 × 2×10-15 × 1² × 2×109 = 4 W.

I5. Design static CMOS gates for (a) Y = ((A + B)C)′ and (b) Y = (AB + C)′.

(a) PDN: (A ∥ B) in series with C. PUN: (A series B) in parallel with C. 6 transistors.

(b) PDN: (A series B) in parallel with C. PUN: (A ∥ B) in series with C. 6 transistors.

I6. How many transistors for a 4-input NOR in static CMOS and in pseudo-nMOS? Give one advantage and two disadvantages of pseudo-nMOS.

Static CMOS 8; pseudo-nMOS 5. Advantage: fewer transistors and less input capacitance (and no slow 4-high pMOS stack). Disadvantages: static current whenever the output is low; VOL > 0 (ratioed), so smaller noise margin.

I7. Your notebook photo: Av = 100. What output change does a 2 mV input change cause at the switching point? Why is high gain good for logic?

|ΔVout| = 100 × 2 mV = 0.2 V (in the opposite direction). High gain means a narrow transition region, so wide noise margins and quick restoration of degraded levels.

I8. List the static properties of the CMOS inverter and explain each in one line.

(1) Zero static power: one transistor is always off. (2) High input impedance: the input is an insulated gate. (3) Low output impedance: the output is always tied to a rail through an ON device. (4) Full swing, ratioless: VOH = VDD, VOL = 0 regardless of sizing.

J. Unit I and MOS extras (Chapter 13)

J1. Estimate ni of Si at 400 K (Nc, Nv ∝ T3/2, Eg = 1.12 eV).

√(NcNv) at 400 K = 1.71×1019 × (4/3)1.5 = 2.63×1019. kT = 0.0345 eV; Eg/2kT = 16.25 ⇒ e−16.25 = 8.8×10-8. ni ≈ 2.3×1012 cm-3, about 300 times its 300 K value.

J2. Si doped with P (Ec − ED = 45 meV) at ND = 1016, 300 K. What fraction of donors is ionised?

Ec − EF ≈ 0.206 eV, so EF − ED = 0.045 − 0.206 = −0.161 eV. Fraction = 1/(1 + 2e−0.161/0.0259) = 1/(1 + 0.004) = 99.6 %: full ionisation is a good assumption.

J3. Cut-off wavelength of Ge (0.66 eV). Can a Si photodiode detect 1.55 μm light?

Ge: 1.24/0.66 = 1.88 μm. Si cuts off at 1.1 μm, so no: 1.55 μm photons (0.8 eV) are below its gap. That is why Ge or InGaAs detectors are used for fibre-optic light.

J4. After the light is switched off, Δn falls to 10 % of its value in 4.6 μs. Find τ.

e−t/τ = 0.1 ⇒ t = τ ln 10 ⇒ τ = 4.6/2.303 = 2 μs.

J5. A p⁺n diode's 1/C² (per area²) vs V plot has slope −1.2×1015 cm⁴F-2V-1 and meets the V axis at 0.8 V. Find ND and Vbi.

ND = 2/(qεs|slope|) = 2/(1.6×10-19 × 1.04×10-12 × 1.2×1015) = 1.0×1016 cm-3; Vbi = 0.8 V.

J6. The forward current of a diode rises ×10 for every 120 mV. Find η and the dominant current mechanism.

Ideal is 59.6 mV/decade (η = 1). 120/59.6 = η ≈ 2 ⇒ recombination current in the depletion region dominates (typical at low forward bias).

J7. Repeat the BJT example of §13.16 with the base width halved (W = 0.5 μm). What happens to β?

B = 1/cosh(0.05) = 0.99875; γ = 1 − 0.0005 = 0.9995 (W also enters γ). α ≈ 0.99825 ⇒ β ≈ 570 (vs 166). A thinner base sharply raises β, at the cost of a lower Early voltage and easier punch-through.

J8. A red LED emits at 620 nm. Estimate its band gap and turn-on voltage.

Eg = 1242/620 = 2.0 eV; turn-on ≈ 1.8 to 2.0 V (close to Eg/q, slightly below).

J9. For NA = 1017 cm-3, find φF and the band bending at the onset of strong inversion. Describe the surface at that point.

φF = 0.0259 ln(1017/1.5×1010) = 0.407 V; ψs = 2φF = 0.81 V. The surface electron density equals the bulk hole density (1017): the surface is as n-type as the bulk is p-type.

J10. Explain why a GaN diode leaks far less in reverse than a Si diode, and why a GaN power device can block more voltage in the same thickness.

Reverse current ∝ ni² (diffusion) and ∝ ni (generation); ni ∝ e−Eg/2kT is astronomically smaller for 3.4 eV than for 1.1 eV. Blocking: VBR = ½WFcrit, and Fcrit is ~3 MV/cm for GaN vs 0.4 MV/cm for Si, so ~7× more voltage for the same W.

K. Manufacturing (Chapter 14)

K1. A plane cuts the axes at x = 2, y = 3 and is parallel to z. Miller indices?

(2, 3, ∞) → (½, ⅓, 0) → ×6 → (3 2 0).

K2. With A = 0.165 μm and B = 0.0117 μm²/h, how long does a 0.2 μm oxide take? How much silicon is consumed?

t = (x² + Ax)/B = (0.04 + 0.033)/0.0117 = 6.2 h. Si consumed = 0.44 × 0.2 = 88 nm.

K3. Give the reactions for dry and wet oxidation and say which is used for the gate oxide and why.

Dry: Si + O2 → SiO2. Wet: Si + 2H2O → SiO2 + 2H2. Dry for the gate: slower but denser, fewer defects and traps, better breakdown. Wet is for thick field oxides where speed matters.

K4. A boron implant: dose 5×1014 cm-2, ΔRp = 0.05 μm. Peak concentration? Why must it be annealed?

Cp = 5×1014/(2.507 × 5×10-6) = 4.0×1019 cm-3. Annealing repairs the lattice damage from nuclear stopping and moves the B atoms onto substitutional sites where they are electrically active.

K5. (a) Resolution of 248 nm lithography with NA = 0.8, k1 = 0.35. (b) What NA is needed for 50 nm features at 193 nm with k1 = 0.3? Is that possible in air?

(a) R = 0.35 × 248/0.8 = 109 nm. (b) NA = 0.3 × 193/50 = 1.16 > 1, impossible in air (n = 1); it needs immersion (water, n = 1.44).

K6. Pre-deposition vs drive-in: boundary condition, profile, and what happens to the surface concentration and dose in each.

Pre-dep: constant surface concentration Cs (unlimited source), erfc profile, dose grows as √t. Drive-in: fixed dose (source removed), Gaussian profile, surface concentration falls as 1/√t while the junction deepens.

K7. In the NMOS process, what do HF and piranha do, and how many masks are used?

HF etches SiO2 through the resist openings; piranha (H2SO4 + H2O2) strips the photoresist. Four masks: S/D windows, gate region, contact openings, metal pattern.

K8. In CZ growth, what sets the ingot diameter, why are the crucible and seed counter-rotated, and what are the two main contaminants?

The pull rate (and melt temperature) sets the diameter. Counter-rotation stirs the melt for a uniform temperature (no hot/cold spots) and uniform doping. Contaminants: oxygen from the quartz crucible and carbon from the graphite parts.

K9. CVD vs PVD: one difference in mechanism and one in step coverage. Name three epitaxy methods.

CVD: gases react chemically on the surface; conformal, good step coverage. PVD: a solid is vaporised (evaporation, sputtering) and condenses; line-of-sight, shadowing. Epitaxy: VPE, LPE, MBE.

M. Semiconductor memories (Chapter 16)

M1. A 4 Mbit square array: rows, columns, row and column address bits.

222 bits = 211 × 211 ⇒ 2048 × 2048; 11 + 11 address bits.

M2. In the board's 4×4 NOR ROM, what word is read when R2 is selected? Where would you add a transistor to make R2 read 0001?

R2 has transistors at C1 and C2 ⇒ 0011. For 0001 add a transistor at (R2, C3).

M3. A DRAM bit line is 300 fF, VDD = 1.2 V, precharge 0.6 V. What cell capacitance gives a 100 mV read signal?

0.6 × CS/(CS + 300) = 0.1 ⇒ 0.5CS = 30 ⇒ CS = 60 fF.

M4. Compare SRAM and DRAM on cell size, refresh, speed and use. Why is the DRAM read destructive?

SRAM: 6T, no refresh, fastest, caches. DRAM: 1T1C, needs refresh, slower, densest, main memory. Reading connects CS to the bit line and its charge is shared away, so the sense amplifier must rewrite it.

M5. Classify: mask ROM, PROM, EPROM, EEPROM, Flash, SRAM, DRAM as volatile or non-volatile, and give the one-line difference between EPROM and EEPROM.

Non-volatile: mask ROM, PROM, EPROM, EEPROM, Flash. Volatile: SRAM, DRAM. EPROM is erased with UV light (whole chip); EEPROM is erased electrically (byte by byte).

N. Explain-in-words questions (viva / short-answer)

N1. Why is the Fermi level called an "imaginary construct"?

Because it is just the energy at which f(E) = ½. There need not be any allowed state at that energy; in a semiconductor EF usually sits inside the forbidden gap.

N2. Why is there no net current at thermal equilibrium even though carriers keep diffusing and drifting?

At every point the diffusion current is exactly cancelled by an equal and opposite drift current (in the junction: Idiffusion = Idrift = Is). This is equivalent to EF being flat throughout the device.

N3. What two things must be true for a hole to be a useful concept?

The valence band must be nearly full (so tracking the few empty states is simpler), and the empty state must respond to fields like a particle with positive charge and positive effective mass. Both hold in a semiconductor.

N4. Why does the depletion region penetrate more into the lightly doped side?

The uncovered charge on both sides must be equal: q ND xn = q NA xp. Fewer ions per unit volume on the light side, so a larger thickness is needed to hold the same total charge.

N5. Why does a MOSFET need almost no gate current?

The gate is separated from the channel by an insulating oxide, forming a capacitor. Only charging current flows while the gate voltage changes; in DC the oxide blocks current. Field effect: the field, not a current, controls the channel.

N7. Why is the pull-up made of pMOS and the pull-down of nMOS, and not the other way round?

An nMOS turns off when its source rises to VDD − Vtn, so it can only pass a weak 1; a pMOS can only pass a weak 0. Putting pMOS to VDD and nMOS to GND lets each pass the level it passes strongly, giving full-swing outputs.

N8. Why was constant-voltage scaling preferred, and what problem does it create?

Supply voltages are standards shared by many chips, so they could not drop every generation. Keeping V fixed while shrinking dimensions raises the electric fields (×S) and power density (×S³): velocity saturation, hot carriers, DIBL, oxide breakdown and heat.

N9. Why does the oxide grow at the Si/SiO2 interface, and why does growth slow down?

The oxidant must diffuse through the oxide to reach silicon, where it reacts. As the oxide thickens the diffusion path lengthens, so the flux falls: growth changes from linear (reaction-limited) to parabolic (diffusion-limited).

N10. Why does ion implantation need an anneal but diffusion does not?

Implanted ions arrive with high energy, knock Si atoms out of place (crystal damage) and mostly stop in interstitial sites, where they are inactive. Annealing repairs the lattice and makes them substitutional. Diffusion happens slowly at high temperature, near equilibrium, so atoms already sit on lattice sites.

N11. Why must DRAM be refreshed but SRAM need not be?

DRAM stores the bit as charge on a tiny capacitor that leaks away through the junction and the off transistor in milliseconds. SRAM stores it in a feedback loop of two inverters, which actively restores the levels as long as power is on.

N12. Name three reasons silicon dominates over Ge and GaAs for VLSI.

(1) It grows an excellent native oxide (SiO2) with a near-perfect interface. (2) Its 1.1 eV gap keeps leakage low at operating temperature (Ge leaks). (3) Abundant, cheap, mechanically strong, available as huge defect-free CZ wafers.

Final pep talk, like I'm 10

Every device in this course is just one idea repeated: carriers are pushed (drift) or spread (diffusion), and something blocks or opens their path. In the diode the blocker is the depletion region; in the BJT it is a thin base; in the MOSFET it is a gate-controlled channel; in CMOS logic it is one of two doors that is always shut; and in the factory every step just decides where those carriers and barriers end up. If you can draw the band picture and say who is pushing whom, you can answer nearly any question.
