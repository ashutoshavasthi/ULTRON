11.1 Two electric fields inside every MOSFET

Your notes (p.11) draw two arrows inside the device:

Ey: the vertical (transverse) field

Made by the gate, pointing down into the substrate. It creates and holds the channel. Ey ∝ VGS/tox.

Ex: the horizontal (longitudinal) field

Made by the drain, pointing along the channel. It pushes the electrons from source to drain. Ex ∝ VDS/L.

For a long-channel device, Ex ≪ Ey. This is the gradual channel approximation (GCA): the channel charge at each point x is set only by the vertical field there, so we can write Q(x) = W Cox(VGS − Vth − V(x)) exactly as you did on p.8. The Drive notes add a second simplification, the charge-sheet approximation: the inversion layer is treated as having zero thickness. Both hold for long channels and break down when L shrinks to about 0.1 μm, which is where the short-channel effects of §11.8 begin.

Explain like I'm 10

The gate is a magnet held above a road: it decides how many cars (electrons) are on the road. The drain is a slope along the road: it decides how fast they roll. As long as the road is long, the slope is gentle and the magnet is in charge. Make the road very short and the slope becomes a cliff: now the drain starts bossing the cars around too, and the simple formulas stop working.

11.2 From the triode formula to saturation

Start from your boxed equation ① (p.8):

ID = μnCox [[W|L]] [ (VGS − Vth)VDS − [[VDS²|2]] ]   ①

Fix VGS and raise VDS. The formula is a downward parabola in VDS. Your notes (p.9) find its peak by setting the slope to zero:

  1. [[dID|dVDS]] = μnCox(W/L)[(VGS − Vth) − VDS] = 0
  2. ⇒ VDS,sat = VGS − Vth (the saturation voltage, equal to the overdrive).
  3. Put it back into ①: (VGS − Vth)(VGS − Vth) − (VGS − Vth)²/2 = ½(VGS − Vth)².
Saturation current (your p.9)
ID,max = ID,sat = [[1|2]] μnCox [[W|L]] (VGS − Vth)²
For VDS ≥ VGS − Vth the current stays at this value: constant, independent of VDS.

Why it stays flat (and doesn't follow the parabola back down): at VDS = VGS − Vth the gate-to-channel voltage at the drain end is exactly Vth, so the channel just disappears there: pinch-off. Raising VDS further only moves the pinch-off point a tiny bit toward the source; the voltage across the remaining channel stays at VGS − Vth, so the current through it stays the same. Electrons reaching the pinch-off point are swept across the short depletion gap to the drain by its strong field. The falling half of the parabola is not physical.

Effect of VGS (p.9): a bigger VGS gives a bigger overdrive, so both VDS,sat and the plateau rise. Your sketch shows VGS = 2 V and 4 V; with Vth = 1 V the plateaus are in the ratio (3/1)² = 9 : 1, not 2 : 1. Doubling VGS does not double the current; the overdrive is what gets squared.

Explain like I'm 10

A funnel feeding a bottle. Tilting the funnel more (raising VDS) makes water run faster, until the neck of the funnel is the bottleneck. After that, tilting more changes nothing: the neck (the pinch-off point) lets through a fixed amount. To get more water you need a wider neck, and that is what a bigger VGS does.

How to decide the region in an exam
  1. Is VGS > Vth? If no: cut-off (ID ≈ 0, or sub-threshold if asked).
  2. Compute the overdrive Vov = VGS − Vth.
  3. Compare VDS with Vov: smaller ⇒ triode (use ①); equal or bigger ⇒ saturation.
  4. If the question gives a circuit and you don't know VDS yet: assume saturation, compute ID, find VDS, then check VDS ≥ Vov. If the check fails, redo with ①.

11.4 The on-resistance Ron

Your p.10 drops the VDS²/2 term in the deep-triode region and gets equation ②, which is Ohm's law:

On-resistance (your p.10; Drive MOSFET_2)
Ron = [[VDS|ID]] = [[1|μnCox(W/L)(VGS − Vth)]]
Ron ∝ 1/(overdrive) and ∝ L/W. Change VGS and the resistance changes: a voltage-variable resistor.

This is the resistance in the "switch model" of Chapter 12 and in the delay τ = RonC. It is why designers want a high mobility (nMOS is better than pMOS), a thin oxide (big Cox), a short L and a large overdrive.

Worked: μnCox = 200 μA/V², W/L = 5, Vth = 0.5 V. Find Ron for VGS = 1.0 V and for VGS = 1.8 V. What VGS gives 1 kΩ?
  1. k = μnCox(W/L) = 200 × 5 = 1000 μA/V² = 1 mA/V².
  2. VGS = 1.0 V: overdrive 0.5 V ⇒ Ron = 1/(10-3 × 0.5) = 2 kΩ.
  3. VGS = 1.8 V: overdrive 1.3 V ⇒ Ron = 1/(1.3×10-3) = 769 Ω.
  4. For 1 kΩ: overdrive = 1/(k × 1000) = 1 V ⇒ VGS = 1.5 V.

Check of the "small VDS" assumption: this Ron is only valid while VDS stays well below the overdrive (say below 0.1 V here).

11.5 Sub-threshold conduction

Below Vth the channel is in weak inversion: there are few electrons, and they move mainly by diffusion (not drift) from the source, over a potential barrier, to the drain, just like electrons crossing a forward-biased p–n junction. That is why the current is exponential:

Sub-threshold current (your p.9; Drive LTSpice notes)
ID,sub ∝ exp[ [[VGS − Vth|n VT]] ] · [ 1 − exp(−VDS/VT) ]
n = ideality (slope) factor, 1 to 1.5    VT = kT/q ≈ 26 mV. The second bracket ≈ 1 once VDS > ~100 mV.
Sub-threshold slope S = n VT ln 10 ≈ n × 60 mV/decade (at 300 K)

Where 60 mV comes from: to raise the current 10×, the exponent must grow by ln 10 = 2.303, so ΔVGS = 2.303 × nVT = 2.303 × 26 mV × n ≈ 60n mV. The Drive notes define S exactly this way: "gate voltage needed to change ID by one decade (e.g. 10-2 → 10-3 A)", ideal 60 mV/decade. No MOSFET at room temperature can switch more sharply than this, because it is set by the thermal spread of electron energies.

Explain like I'm 10

A tap that never closes completely. Turning the handle below the "off" mark makes the drip slower and slower, ten times slower for every 60 mV, but it never reaches exactly zero. A chip has billions of these dripping taps, which is why a phone's battery drains slowly even when the screen is off. This drip is a big part of static power.

Worked: a transistor has S = 80 mV/decade and ID = 1 μA at VGS = Vth. What is the leakage at VGS = 0 if Vth = 0.4 V? What Vth would be needed for 1 pA?
  1. Going from Vth down to 0 is 400 mV = 400/80 = 5 decades.
  2. Ioff = 1 μA × 10-5 = 10 pA.
  3. 1 pA is 6 decades below 1 μA ⇒ Vth = 6 × 80 = 480 mV.

This is the core trade-off of low-power design: a lower Vth gives a faster transistor (more overdrive) but exponentially more leakage.

11.7 Device scaling

Moore's law (the Drive quotes doubling every 1.5 to 2 years) is achieved by scaling: shrinking L, W and tox. Halving each dimension fits four times as many transistors in the same area. The question is what to do with the voltages. Your notes (p.12) list two methods:

1. Constant-field scaling

Shrink the voltages by the same factor as the dimensions, so the fields stay the same. Your example: Ex = VDS/L → (VDS/2)/(L/2) and Ey = VGS/tox → (VGS/3)/(tox/3): unchanged. (The Drive: L 1 → 0.5 μm needs VDS 1 → 0.5 V.)

2. Constant-voltage scaling

Shrink the dimensions but keep the supply voltage. Your notes: "generally used"; the Drive: "often preferred". The practical reason: chips must keep talking to other chips at standard voltages (5 V, 3.3 V, 1.8 V), so the supply can't fall freely.

Constant-voltage scaling is faster but raises the fields by S, which causes velocity saturation, hot carriers, DIBL and oxide breakdown, and raises power density by S³. Real industry did constant-voltage scaling until about the 1990s and then lowered VDD slowly ("general scaling").

Worked: a 1 μm process with VDD = 5 V and tox = 20 nm is scaled to 0.5 μm. Give tox, VDD, current, delay and power per transistor under each method.

S = 2. Both methods: tox = 10 nm, gate capacitance halves, area quarters.

Constant field: VDD = 2.5 V; ID ×½; delay ×½; power ×¼; power density unchanged.

Constant voltage: VDD = 5 V; ID ×2; delay ×¼; power ×2; power density ×8; fields ×2 (now the short-channel effects below bite).

11.8 Second-order and short-channel effects

These are the five effects listed in your notes (p.12 to p.14), in the same order, with the Drive's explanations added.

{{fig:mos_sce|Left: channel-length modulation (the saturated lines extrapolate back to −VA = −1/λ). Middle: velocity saturation. Right: drain-induced barrier lowering.}}

1. Body effect

The substrate (body, B) is the fourth terminal. Your notes: nMOS body → most negative potential; pMOS body → most positive potential. Reason (Drive): the source–body and drain–body p–n junctions must stay reverse-biased; if the nMOS body went positive they would forward-bias, a large current would flow and the device could burn out.

If the source sits above the body (VSB > 0, e.g. the upper transistor in a series stack), the source–body junction is more reverse-biased, the depletion region under the channel is thicker and holds more negative charge, so the gate must work harder to invert the surface: Vth rises.

Body effect
Vth = Vth0 + γ ( √(2φF + VSB) − √(2φF) )    γ = [[√(2qεsNA)|Cox]]
Worked: Vth0 = 0.5 V, γ = 0.4 V½, 2φF = 0.7 V. Find Vth for VSB = 1 V and 2 V.

VSB = 1 V: Vth = 0.5 + 0.4(√1.7 − √0.7) = 0.5 + 0.4(1.304 − 0.837) = 0.5 + 0.187 = 0.687 V.

VSB = 2 V: 0.5 + 0.4(√2.7 − 0.837) = 0.5 + 0.4(1.643 − 0.837) = 0.822 V. The rise is sub-linear (square root).

2. Channel-length modulation (CLM)

In saturation the pinch-off point moves toward the source as VDS rises (the drain depletion region "eats into" the channel), so the effective length becomes L − ΔL. A shorter channel carries more current (ID ∝ 1/L, your p.13), so the saturated curves tilt upward instead of being flat:

CLM (your p.13)
ID = [[1|2]] μnCox [[W|L]] (VGS − Vth)² (1 + λVDS)
λ = CLM parameter (V-1)    VA = 1/λ (the MOSFET's "Early voltage")    ro = 1/(λID) = finite output resistance

The device is no longer an ideal current source. λ is larger (worse) for shorter channels, because the same ΔL is a bigger fraction of a small L. This is the exact twin of base-width modulation and the Early effect in the BJT (your set-A p.36).

Worked: an nMOS in saturation carries 1 mA at VDS = 1 V with λ = 0.1 V-1. Find the current at VDS = 3 V, VA and ro.
  1. 1 mA = I0(1 + 0.1 × 1) ⇒ I0 = 0.909 mA.
  2. At 3 V: ID = 0.909 × 1.3 = 1.18 mA (18 % more).
  3. VA = 1/λ = 10 V; ro ≈ 1/(λID) = 1/(0.1 × 1 mA) = 10 kΩ.

3. Velocity saturation

Recall Chapter 6: drift velocity vd = μE only at low fields; at high field it levels off at vsat (≈ 107 cm/s in Si) because scattering (optical phonons) balances the push. In a short channel, Ex = VDS/L is huge even for small VDS, so carriers hit vsat long before pinch-off. Your notes (p.13 to p.14):

Velocity saturation (your p.14)
vd = [[μE|1 + E/Ec]] for E < Ec;   vd = vsat for E > Ec    (Ec = critical field, vsat ≈ μEc)
ID,short = μnCox[[W|L]][(VGS − Vth)VDS − [[VDS²|2]]] × [[1|1 + VDS/(EcL)]]
Deep velocity saturation: ID ≈ W Cox vsat (VGS − Vth − VDSAT): linear in the overdrive, not squared.

Consequences (Drive MOSFET_3): VDSAT < VGS − Vth (the device saturates early), and ID becomes linear in (VGS − Vth) and almost independent of L. Shrinking L no longer increases the current as much as the long-channel formula promises.

Worked: μn = 400 cm²/V·s in the channel, vsat = 107 cm/s. Find Ec, and the current-reduction factor at VDS = 1 V for L = 1 μm and L = 100 nm.
  1. Ec = vsat/μ = 107/400 = 2.5×104 V/cm (2.5 V/μm).
  2. L = 1 μm: EcL = 2.5 V ⇒ factor 1/(1 + 1/2.5) = 0.71.
  3. L = 100 nm: EcL = 0.25 V ⇒ factor 1/(1 + 1/0.25) = 0.20: the current is only one fifth of the long-channel prediction.

4. Punch-through

The source–substrate and drain–substrate depletion regions grow toward each other. In a very short channel (or at high VDS) they touch, creating a direct path for electrons from source to drain under the gate. Current flows even when the device should be OFF, and the gate can no longer stop it. Remedy (Drive, doping notes): higher channel doping shortens the depletion widths and suppresses punch-through (and DIBL).

5. Drain-induced barrier lowering (DIBL)

Electrons in the source face a potential barrier (the hump in the right panel of the figure) that the gate controls. In a long channel only the gate sets this barrier. In a short channel the drain is close enough that a high VDS also pulls the barrier down. Lower barrier ⇒ easier to turn on ⇒ Vth falls as VDS rises (your p.14), so off-state leakage rises.

Beyond your notes

DIBL is usually modelled as Vth = Vth0 − ηDIBLVDS, with ηDIBL ≈ 0.05 to 0.15 V/V in short devices. Other short-channel effects you may meet: Vth roll-off (Vth falls as L shrinks), hot-carrier injection (energetic carriers damage the oxide), gate-oxide tunnelling (tox below ~2 nm, solved by high-k dielectrics, Chapter 14) and mobility degradation by the vertical field. FinFETs and nanosheet (gate-all-around) transistors wrap the gate around the channel to give the gate back its control.

Explain like I'm 10: the five effects in one story

The gate is a teacher controlling a doorway between two classrooms (source and drain). Body effect: the floor tilts, so the teacher must shout louder to open the door. CLM: the drain side keeps creeping closer, so the corridor gets shorter and a few more kids get through. Velocity saturation: kids can only run so fast, however steep the corridor. DIBL: the corridor is so short that the drain classroom's noise opens the door a little by itself. Punch-through: the two classrooms' walls collapse into each other and kids walk straight through, door or no door.

Exam traps for this chapter
  • Saturation in a MOSFET means current saturates (flat). In a BJT, "saturation" is the region where both junctions are forward-biased and the transistor is a closed switch. Don't mix them.
  • VDS,sat = VGS − Vth, not VGS.
  • Body effect: VSB ↑ ⇒ Vth ↑. DIBL: VDS ↑ ⇒ Vth ↓. Opposite directions, both asked.
  • CLM makes the curves slope up in saturation; it doesn't change the triode region formula (to first order).
  • Your p.14 writes the velocity-saturation factor as 1/(1 + VDS/Ec). Write 1/(1 + VDS/(EcL)): VDS/L is the field.
  • The sub-threshold slope can never be below 60 mV/decade at 300 K for a normal MOSFET; "lower S" means a better (sharper) switch.
