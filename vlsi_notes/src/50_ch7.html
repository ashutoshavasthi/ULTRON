CHAPTER 7The p–n Junction Diode

Glue a p-type piece to an n-type piece and something magical appears: a one-way street for current.

Your notes p.19 to p.30

7.1 Physical structure

A p–n diode is one crystal, p-doped on one side and n-doped on the other, with a metal contact on each end. The p-side terminal is the anode and the n-side terminal is the cathode. The boundary is the metallurgical junction. The circuit symbol arrow points from anode to cathode, the direction conventional current is allowed to flow.

Explain like I'm 10

Two neighbouring playgrounds. Playground P has lots of empty swings (holes). Playground N has lots of kids (electrons). A one-way turnstile in the fence lets kids pass in only one direction. That fence-with-turnstile is the diode.

7.2 Open circuit: what happens the instant p meets n

{{fig:pn_structure|Your pages 19 to 22: carriers, fixed ions, the depletion region, the charge profile and the electric-field profile.}}
  1. Concentration difference. The p-side is full of holes (p ≫ n) and the n-side full of electrons (n ≫ p). Your notes: "p>n" on the left and "n>p" on the right.
  2. Diffusion current ID. Holes diffuse from p to n; electrons diffuse from n to p. Both carriers moving this way make a conventional current from p to n. Your notes: "Diffusion current: p → n".
  3. A depletion region forms. When a hole leaves the p-side it leaves behind a fixed negative acceptor ion; when an electron leaves the n-side it leaves a fixed positive donor ion. Near the junction these uncovered charges pile up: Q− on the p-side, Q+ on the n-side. The region has almost no free carriers, hence depletion region (also called space-charge region).
  4. Built-in electric field. The + and − sheets create a field that points from the n-side to the p-side. This field is a hill (barrier) that opposes further diffusion. The voltage across it is the barrier potential (built-in voltage) V0.
  5. Drift current Idrift. The same field sweeps minority carriers across: electrons from the p-side to the n-side, holes from the n-side to the p-side. Drift current flows from n to p (conventional). Your notes: "Drift current is due to the movement of minority charge carriers"; it is also called the saturation current Is. Majority carriers: p on the p-side, n on the n-side. Minority: n on the p-side, p on the n-side.
  6. Equilibrium (open circuit). Diffusion grows until the barrier is high enough that the two currents are equal and opposite:
Equilibrium
Idiffusion = Idrift = Is ⇒ net current = 0
Explain like I'm 10

Kids from the N playground run into the P playground. But every kid who leaves makes the N side more "positive" (missing kids' friends) and every swing that gets filled makes the P side "negative". Soon there's an invisible wall of charge at the fence that pushes new kids back. When the pushback exactly equals the urge to run over, the traffic stops. That wall is the depletion region; its strength is V0.

7.3 The built-in voltage V0

Junction built-in voltage
V0 = VT ln( [[ NA ND | ni² ]] )
VT = kBT/q (thermal voltage), NA, ND = doping, ni = intrinsic concentration
Beyond your notes: one-line derivation

At equilibrium the electron concentration on the n-side (nn0 ≈ ND) and on the p-side (np0 = ni²/NA) differ by the Boltzmann factor of the barrier: nn0/np0 = eV0/VT. Therefore V0 = VT ln(NDNA/ni²).

  • Typical Si diodes: V0 ≈ 0.6 to 0.8 V; Ge: about 0.3 V. Heavier doping ⇒ larger V0 (only logarithmically).
  • You cannot measure V0 with a voltmeter across the open diode: the metal contacts create equal and opposite contact potentials.

7.4 Width of the depletion region and stored charge

Let the depletion region extend xp into the p-side and xn into the n-side, so the total width is W = xn + xp (your notes use ω).

Step 1: the two charge sheets are equal (charge neutrality of the whole diode)

|Q+| = q (A xn) ND    |Q−| = q (A xp) NA    (A = cross-section area)
|Q+| = |Q−| ⇒ [[ xn | xp ]] = [[ NA | ND ]]
Explain like I'm 10

A see-saw again: the two sides must carry equal "weights". If one side has bricks packed 100× more densely, it only needs a layer 1/100 as thick. So the depletion region sinks deeper into the lightly doped side. If NA ≫ ND (a p⁺–n junction), then xn ≫ xp, and W ≈ xn.

Step 2: the width (given in your notes)

Depletion width (zero bias)
W = xn + xp = √( [[ 2εs | q ]] ( [[1 | NA]] + [[1 | ND]] ) V0 )
εs = permittivity of the semiconductor; for Si: 1.04×10-12 F/cm (= 11.7 ε0)

Step 3: splitting W into xn and xp (your derivation)

  1. From xn/xp = NA/ND, add 1 to both sides: (xp + xn)/xp = (ND + NA)/ND.
  2. Since xp + xn = W:
xp = W · [[ ND | NA + ND ]]     xn = W · [[ NA | NA + ND ]]

Notice the "crossover": xp carries ND (the other side's doping) in the numerator. A great way to remember it: the region on the lightly doped side is the wider one.

Step 4: charge stored on either side

Junction charge QJ = |Q+| = |Q−|
QJ = A q ( [[ NA ND | NA + ND ]] ) W
QJ = A √( 2 εs q ( [[ NA ND | NA + ND ]] ) V0 )
Worked (typical exam problem): a Si p–n junction has NA = 1018, ND = 1016 cm-3, ni = 1.5×1010, A = 10-4 cm², T = 300 K. Find V0, W, xn, xp, QJ. Then find W when 5 V reverse bias is applied.
  1. V0 = 0.0259 × ln(1018×1016 / 2.25×1020) = 0.0259 × ln(4.44×1013) = 0.0259 × 31.43 = 0.814 V.
  2. W = √[ (2×1.04×10-12 / 1.6×10-19) × (10-18 + 10-16) × 0.814 ] = √[1.3×107 × 1.01×10-16 × 0.814] = 3.27×10-5 cm = 0.327 μm.
  3. xn = W NA/(NA+ND) = 0.327 × 0.9901 = 0.323 μm, xp = 0.327 × 0.0099 = 0.0032 μm. Almost the whole region is on the lightly doped n-side, as predicted.
  4. QJ = A q [NAND/(NA+ND)] W = 10-4 × 1.6×10-19 × 9.9×1015 × 3.27×10-5 ≈ 5.2×10-12 C (5.2 pC).
  5. Reverse bias VR = 5 V: replace V0 by V0 + VR = 5.814 V. W scales with √(barrier): W = 0.327 × √(5.814/0.814) = 0.327 × 2.673 = 0.873 μm.

7.5 Energy-band diagram of the p–n junction

{{fig:pn_bands|Your pages 23 to 24. E_F is flat (no net current) while E_c and E_v bend across the depletion region (electric field).}}
  1. Far from the junction, the p-side has EF near Ev, the n-side has EF near Ec.
  2. At equilibrium EF must be one flat line across the device (your note: "EF const ⇒ no current in the device").
  3. To keep EF flat, the p-side bands sit higher than the n-side bands. The bands bend through the depletion region. The bending is qV0 (your note: "slope in Ec and Ev ⇒ an electric field exists").
  4. Electrons on the n-side see a hill of height qV0 if they try to go to the p-side, and likewise holes.

Field from the band bending (your derivation)

Field: 𝓔 = − dV/dx  (1)    Electron energy: Ec = −qV ⇒ V = −Ec/q  (2)
Substitute (2) in (1): 𝓔 = [[1 | q]] · [[ dEc | dx ]]

The steeper Ec(x), the stronger the field. Zero slope ⇒ zero field (as in the neutral regions).

Explain like I'm 10

Forward: you lower the wall at the fence, so lots of kids pour across, and the current explodes as the wall gets lower. Reverse: you raise the wall even higher. Now nobody can cross except a rare few kids who are pushed across by the wall itself (the minority carriers), which gives a tiny, constant leakage current.

With reverse bias VR (in your notes)
W = √( [[ 2εs | q ]] ( [[1 | NA]] + [[1 | ND]] ) (V0 + VR) )
QJ = A √( 2 εs q [[ NA ND | NA + ND ]] (V0 + VR) )
(For forward bias replace V0 + VR by V0 − VF.)
Beyond your notes: junction capacitance

Because QJ changes with VR, a reverse-biased diode acts as a voltage-controlled capacitor: Cj = dQJ/dVR = εs A / W = Cj0 / √(1 + VR/V0). Wider W ⇒ smaller C. Used in varactor (tuning) diodes.

7.7 The current–voltage relation (deriving the diode equation)

Your notes derive this in five moves. Follow the story:

  1. Lower the barrier. Forward bias V reduces the barrier to V0 − V.
  2. Majority carriers diffuse over (electrons n → p side, holes p → n side).
  3. They become minority carriers on the other side: "injection of minority carriers". Let np0 = equilibrium electron concentration on the p-side (np0 = ni²/NA) and pn0 = ni²/ND on the n-side.
  4. Concentration right at the edge of the depletion region is boosted by the Boltzmann factor:
Edge-of-depletion-region concentrations (steady state)
pn(xn) = pn0 eV/VT     np(−xp) = np0 eV/VT
Excess (injected) holes at x = xn: pn0 eV/VT − pn0 = pn0(eV/VT − 1)
Excess electrons at x = −xp: np0(eV/VT − 1)
Explain like I'm 10

Each 60 mV (about 2.3 VT) of forward voltage multiplies the number of kids sneaking over by 10. So a small voltage change makes a huge change in the flow. That is why the forward curve is so steep.

  1. The injected carriers recombine as they wander deeper, so the excess decays exponentially with the diffusion length L:
{{fig:excess_carriers|Your pages 26 to 27: injected minority-carrier profiles. Decay due to recombination.}}
pn(x) = pn0 + pn0(eV/VT − 1) e−(x − xn)/Lp
np(x) = np0 + np0(eV/VT − 1) e−x′/Ln (x′ = distance measured away from the junction edge at −xp; your notes write it as (x − xp))
Lp, Ln = diffusion lengths of holes and electrons
  1. Current from the gradient. Differentiate: dp/dx = −(1/Lp) × (excess) at the edge, so Jp = −q Dp dp/dx gives:
Jp(xn) = q [[ Dp | Lp ]] pn0 (eV/VT − 1)     Jn(−xp) = q [[ Dn | Ln ]] np0 (eV/VT − 1)
  1. Total current = hole part + electron part, both evaluated at the depletion edges (total current is the same everywhere in steady state; away from the junction it is carried by drift in the quasi-neutral regions, Jp = pqμp𝓔, Jn = nqμn𝓔):
I = A Jp + A Jn = A q ( [[ Dp | Lp ]] pn0 + [[ Dn | Ln ]] np0 ) (eV/VT − 1)
Put np0 = ni²/NA, pn0 = ni²/ND:
I = Is ( eV/VT − 1 )    with   Is = A q ni² ( [[ Dp | Lp ND ]] + [[ Dn | Ln NA ]] )
{{fig:diode_iv|Your page 28. The V=0 point, the flat reverse current −I_s, the exponential forward rise, and the breakdown knee.}}

Reading the I–V curve (your three cases)

  • V = 0: ID = Is(1 − 1) = 0 A.
  • V = −1, −2, −3 V (reverse): once |V| ≫ VT, eV/VT → 0 and ID = −Is, independent of voltage. That is why Is is called the saturation current.
  • V increasing (forward): I ≈ Is eV/VT, exponential. The diode appears to "turn on" around 0.6–0.7 V in silicon because Is is so tiny (~10-15 A) that noticeable current needs V near 0.6 V.
Exam traps
  • Depletion-region diffusion current depends on the majority carriers climbing a hill; drift current on the minority carriers rolling down. Don't swap them.
  • The saturation current formula has the other side's doping crossed: the hole term uses ND (n-side donors), the electron term uses NA.
  • In the formula, use VT = 25.9 mV (or 26 mV), never 0.6 V.
  • Is doubles roughly every 5 °C in silicon (because ni² rises so fast). At a fixed current, the forward voltage falls about 2 mV per °C.

7.8 Breakdown region

Push the reverse voltage far enough and the current suddenly shoots up at the breakdown voltage VBR. It is not necessarily destructive; the junction is destroyed only if the power gets too large. Your notes list two mechanisms:

Zener mechanism

A strong electric field at the junction pulls electrons straight out of covalent bonds (quantum tunnelling). Happens in heavily doped junctions with a very thin depletion region. VBR < ≈ 5 V.

Avalanche mechanism

Carriers gain high kinetic energy from the field, collide with atoms, knock out new electron–hole pairs, which are themselves accelerated: a chain reaction. Happens in lightly doped, wide junctions. VBR > ≈ 8 V.

Between about 5 and 8 V both mechanisms contribute. Zener diodes (voltage references) use the controlled breakdown.

Explain like I'm 10

Zener: the wall is so thin and the push so strong that kids are yanked straight through the wall. Avalanche: one kid running fast crashes into two people, who then run fast and crash into four, and so on, like a snowball rolling downhill.

7.9 Your worked diode question (re-solved with checks)

A p–n diode has NA = 1018 cm-3, ND = 1016 cm-3, A = 10-4 cm², ni = 1.5×1010 cm-3, Lp = 5 μm, Ln = 10 μm, Dp = 10 cm²/s, Dn = 18 cm²/s. It is forward biased and conducts ID = 0.1 mA. Calculate (i) Is, (ii) the forward-bias voltage, (iii) the hole-injection and electron-injection components of I.

(i) Saturation current. Convert lengths: Lp = 5×10-4 cm, Ln = 10×10-4 cm. ni² = 2.25×1020.

Hole term: Dp/(LpND) = 10 / (5×10-4 × 1016) = 10/5×1012 = 2×10-12
Electron term: Dn/(LnNA) = 18 / (10-3 × 1018) = 1.8×10-14
A q ni² = 10-4 × 1.6×10-19 × 2.25×1020 = 3.6×10-3
Is = 3.6×10-3 × (2×10-12 + 1.8×10-14) = 7.26×10-15 A

(ii) Voltage. I = Is(eV/VT − 1) ⇒ 0.1×10-3 = 7.26×10-15(eV/VT − 1) ⇒ eV/VT = 1.38×1010 ⇒ V = VT ln(1.38×1010) = 0.026 × 23.35 = 0.607 V (your notes: 0.605 V).

(iii) Split the current. Divide the two components:

Ip/In = (2×10-12)/(1.8×10-14) = 111 = [[Dp | Dn]] · [[Ln | Lp]] · [[NA | ND]]
Ip + In = 0.1 mA ⇒ In = 0.1/112 = 0.0009 mA, Ip = 0.0991 mA

Meaning: 99.1 % of the current is hole injection from the heavily doped p-side into the lightly doped n-side. Making one side much heavier makes the diode "one-carrier", which is exactly the trick used for the BJT emitter (Chapter 8). (Your notes wrote Is = 7.26×10-15 A but then typed 10-13 in the next line; the 10-15 value is the one consistent with V = 0.605 V.)

Your assignment: for Si and Ge p–n diodes, which needs the higher voltage to carry a fixed forward current of 20 mA? (Hint: ni ∝ e−Eg/2kBT.)
  1. Eg(Si) = 1.1 eV > Eg(Ge) = 0.7 eV.
  2. A bigger gap ⇒ much smaller ni: ni,Si < ni,Ge.
  3. Is ∝ ni² ⇒ Is,Si ≪ Is,Ge.
  4. For the same I = IseV/VT: V = VT ln(I/Is), so the smaller Is needs the larger V.

Answer: silicon needs the higher voltage. Size of the effect: Is ratio ≈ e−(1.1−0.7)/kBT ≈ 2×10-7 (ignoring the T3 factor and mobility differences), so the voltage difference is VT ln(5×106) ≈ 0.4 V. That matches the well-known turn-on voltages of ≈0.7 V for Si and ≈0.3 V for Ge.
