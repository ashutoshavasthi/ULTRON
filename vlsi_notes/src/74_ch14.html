CHAPTER 14Manufacturing: From Sand to a Working Chip

How the transistors of Chapters 9 to 12 are actually made: crystal growth, oxidation, doping by diffusion and implantation, lithography, thin films and epitaxy, and the complete NMOS process.

Drive: 3_Manufacturing notes (Crystal growth, Oxidation, Doping, Lithography, NMOS steps) Drive: slides M1 to M6
Explain like I'm 10

Making a chip is like printing a city with a very fancy stamp, many times over. First you need a perfect blank page: a slice of pure silicon crystal (made from sand). Then, layer by layer, you grow glass on it (oxidation), sprinkle special atoms into chosen spots (doping), draw the pattern for the next layer with light through a stencil (lithography), carve away what you don't want (etching) and paint on new layers like metal wires (deposition). Repeat 20 to 80 times and you have billions of transistors.

14.1 The big picture (M1, M2)

Design to chip: requirements → hardware description (HDL) → logic gates (synthesis) → layout (place and route) → cleanroom fabrication → testing and packaging.

Fabrication in one line: sand → polysilicon → ingot → slice → polish → [oxidise → photolithography (coat resist → spin → soft-bake → align and expose through the mask → develop) → etch → strip resist → dope] × many layers → metal interconnects (PVD/CVD) → passivation → dicing → packaging and bonding → test.

The five process categories (NMOS notes)Techniques
1. Wafer preparationCZ crystal growth (Bridgman for GaAs, float zone), sawing, lapping, etching, CMP polishing
2. Oxide formationdry or wet thermal oxidation

The steps are not strictly in this order; they repeat and interleave. Wafers are cleaned after almost every step.

14.2 The silicon crystal (M3)

  • Unit cells: simple cubic, body-centred cubic (BCC), face-centred cubic (FCC).
  • Silicon = the diamond lattice: two interpenetrating FCC lattices, one shifted from the other by a/4 along x, y and z. Each atom has 4 nearest neighbours (the 4 covalent bonds of Chapter 4).
  • Miller indices name crystal planes: take the plane's intercepts on x, y, z (in units of a), take reciprocals, and clear fractions to the smallest whole numbers. Wafers are cut along (100) or (111); (100) is used for MOS because it has the fewest interface traps. A direction is written in square brackets, e.g. [111].
  • Defects (all harmful): point defects (vacancy, interstitial), line defects (dislocations), area defects (stacking faults), volume defects (precipitates). Chapter 13's traps come from these.
Worked: a plane cuts the axes at x = 1, y = 2, z = ∞ (parallel to z). Find its Miller indices.

Intercepts (1, 2, ∞) → reciprocals (1, ½, 0) → multiply by 2 → (2 1 0). A plane cutting only x at 1 (parallel to y and z) is (1 0 0); one cutting all three at 1 is (1 1 1).

14.3 From sand to electronic-grade silicon (M3)

  1. Metallurgical-grade Si (MGS), 98 % pure: quartzite sand reduced with carbon in a submerged-electrode arc furnace at ~2000 °C: SiO2 + 2C → Si + 2CO. Impurities Al, Fe. (90 % of MGS goes to the steel industry.)
  2. Trichlorosilane: Si + 3HCl → SiHCl3 + H2. SiHCl3 boils at 32 °C, so it is purified by fractional distillation.
  3. Electronic-grade Si (EGS): in a Siemens CVD reactor, 2SiHCl3 + 2H2 → 2Si + 6HCl deposits polysilicon nuggets with impurities at the parts-per-billion level (mainly B, P, C). EGS is about 99.999999999 % pure ("eleven nines").

14.4 Growing the single crystal: the Czochralski method

{{fig:cz|The Czochralski puller described in your Drive notes and slide M3.}}
  • Invented in 1918; also called the "liquid–solid mono-component growth system". Essentially all IC wafers are CZ wafers. The other method is float zone (higher purity, no crucible); Bridgman is used for GaAs.
  • Parts: quartz (silica) crucible inside a graphite susceptor, graphite heater (RF induction or resistance), a chamber that is evacuated and then filled with argon, a crucible shaft rotating clockwise and a seed/pull shaft rotating anticlockwise holding a seed crystal of orientation ⟨100⟩ or ⟨111⟩.
  • Steps: load the crushed EGS ("the charge"), evacuate, fill with Ar, melt at about 1412 to 1417 °C (Si melts at 1414 °C), dip the seed, then pull slowly while rotating; silicon freezes onto the seed copying its orientation.
  • Control parameters: pull rate (a few mm/min; it sets the diameter: too slow and the seed melts back), melt temperature, and rotation rate (counter-rotation keeps the melt temperature uniform, no hot or cold spots).
  • Product: an ingot 8 to 16 inches (200 to 450 mm) in diameter, sliced into wafers 0.5 to 0.8 mm thick. Bigger wafers mean more chips per run.
  • Disadvantages: oxygen from the quartz crucible (≈ 1018 cm-3; a contaminant, but it also strengthens the wafer) and carbon from the graphite (≈ 1016 cm-3). The crucible cracks on cooling and can't be reused.

14.5 Thermal oxidation

Silicon heated in O2 or steam grows its own glass, SiO2. The Si/SiO2 interface is one of the cleanest electrical interfaces known, which is a key reason silicon (not Ge or GaAs) dominates electronics. SiO2 has a band gap of ~9 eV and a high breakdown field.

Uses of the oxide

  • Gate dielectric (thin, highest quality)
  • Field / isolation oxide (thick; raises the parasitic Vth so no channel forms between neighbouring devices; LOCOS = local oxidation of silicon)
  • Mask against dopants (dopants diffuse far more slowly in SiO2)

Furnace procedure: load a quartz boat of 50 to 100 wafers into a quartz tube furnace (horizontal, or vertical for a smaller footprint and symmetric gas flow) slowly to avoid thermal shock; flush with inert gas; ramp to ~900 to 1200 °C; oxidise; ramp down; pull out slowly. The rate is exponential in temperature (Arrhenius), so temperature uniformity is critical.

Where the oxide grows: at the Si/SiO2 interface, not on top. The oxidant must diffuse through the oxide already there, then react with silicon. Silicon is consumed: the oxide is about 2.2 times the thickness of the silicon it eats, i.e. 44 % of the final oxide thickness is consumed silicon.

Deal–Grove model (M4)
F1 = hG(CG − CS) gas to surface    F2 = D(C* − Ci)/x0 diffusion through the oxide    F3 = ksCi reaction at the interface    steady state F1 = F2 = F3
x0² + A x0 = B(t + τ)    B = parabolic rate constant, B/A = linear rate constant, τ accounts for any initial oxide
Thin oxide (short t): x0 ≈ (B/A)(t + τ), linear, reaction-limited    Thick oxide (long t): x0² ≈ B(t + τ), parabolic, diffusion-limited (Ci ≈ 0)
{{fig:deal_grove|Deal–Grove growth curve with its linear and parabolic limits (A = 0.165 μm, B = 0.0117 μm²/h, typical of dry O2 near 1000 °C).}}
Worked: with A = 0.165 μm, B = 0.0117 μm²/h and no initial oxide, how thick is the oxide after 2 h and after 10 h? How much silicon did the 2 h oxide consume?
  1. Solve the quadratic: x0 = [−A + √(A² + 4Bt)]/2.
  2. t = 2 h: √(0.0272 + 0.0936) = 0.348 ⇒ x0 = (0.348 − 0.165)/2 = 0.091 μm = 91 nm.
  3. t = 10 h: √(0.0272 + 0.468) = 0.704 ⇒ x0 = 0.27 μm. Five times the time gives only three times the oxide: growth slows as the oxide thickens.
  4. Si consumed at 2 h: 0.44 × 91 = 40 nm.
  • Oxide thickness by colour: light reflecting from the top and bottom of the film interferes. Destructive interference when 2noxd = (m + ½)λ removes some colours, so each thickness has a characteristic colour (a colour chart is used for quick checks, e.g. ~1000 Å looks violet).
  • Deal–Grove fails for very thin dry oxides (it underestimates the fast initial growth).
  • The thin-oxide limit: below ~2 nm electrons tunnel through the gate oxide (gate leakage). Modern chips replace SiO2 with high-k (hafnium-based) oxides, deposited by atomic-layer deposition (ALD) rather than grown: Cox = κε0/t, so a larger κ (SiO2 κ ≈ 3.9) gives the same Cox with a physically thicker, non-leaky film.

14.6 Doping by thermal diffusion

Why doping control matters (Drive): source/drain doping sets the parasitic resistance (lower R ⇒ more drive current); channel doping controls short-channel effects (higher doping suppresses punch-through and DIBL, Chapter 11); doping must be controlled vertically and laterally, which is hard in FinFETs and nanosheets.

  • The 1960s–80s method: wafers in a quartz furnace at 800 to 1000 °C (up to ~1200 °C) with a dopant source: POCl3 (phosphorus oxychloride) for n-type, boron sources for p-type. SiO2 windows define where dopant enters.
  • Only substitutional dopants are electrically active (sitting on a Si lattice site); interstitial ones are inactive.
  • Atomic mechanisms: vacancy-assisted (A + V ⇌ AV: the dopant hops into a neighbouring empty site) and interstitialcy / kick-out (A + I ⇌ AI).
  • The maximum concentration that dissolves is the solid solubility limit.
Fick's laws and Arrhenius
1st law: J = −D [[∂C|∂x]]    2nd law: [[∂C|∂t]] = D [[∂²C|∂x²]]    D = D0 exp(−Ea/kT)    diffusion length √(Dt)
(Fick's laws are accurate only at low concentrations; they fail at very high doping and during oxidation.)
{{fig:diffusion_profiles|The two-step diffusion process. Left: pre-deposition, constant surface concentration. Right: drive-in, fixed total dose.}}

Step 1: pre-deposition

Unlimited source at the surface ⇒ surface concentration fixed at Cs (the solubility limit).

C(x,t) = Cs erfc[x/(2√(Dt))]

Dose introduced: Q = 2Cs√(Dt/π) (∝ Cs√(Dt)).

Step 2: drive-in

Source removed; a fixed dose Q spreads deeper and the surface concentration falls.

C(x,t) = [[Q|√(πDt)]] exp[−x²/(4Dt)] (Gaussian)

Junction depth where C = background CB: xj = 2√(Dt ln[Q/(CB√(πDt))]).

Worked: pre-deposition with Cs = 1020 cm-3, D = 10-14 cm²/s for 1 h; then drive-in with D = 5×10-13 cm²/s for 2 h into a substrate with CB = 1016 cm-3. Find the dose, the final surface concentration and the junction depth.
  1. Pre-dep: Dt = 10-14 × 3600 = 3.6×10-11 cm² (√(Dt) = 0.06 μm). Q = 2 × 1020 × √(3.6×10-11/π) = 6.8×1014 cm-2.
  2. Drive-in: Dt = 5×10-13 × 7200 = 3.6×10-9 cm²; √(πDt) = 1.06×10-4 cm. Surface C = Q/√(πDt) = 6.4×1018 cm-3 (it fell from 1020).
  3. xj = 2√(3.6×10-9 × ln(6.4×1018/1016)) = 2√(3.6×10-9 × 6.46) = 3.05×10-4 cm = 3.0 μm.

14.7 Ion implantation

The modern method (since the 1980s): dopant ions are accelerated and fired into the wafer. Precise, done at low temperature, and almost any film can be the mask (metal, thick SiO2, photoresist). The profile peaks below the surface.

The implanter, in beam order (Drive)
  1. Ion source / ioniser: electrons or a plasma ionise the dopant gas.
  2. Mass separator (analysing magnet): bends each ion by its mass/charge; only the wanted species passes the slit.
  3. Accelerator: sets the ion energy, and the energy sets the depth (keV to MeV).
  4. Scanning and focusing: X/Y electrostatic plates sweep the beam over the wafer.
  5. Neutral beam trap: neutral atoms can't be steered, so the beam is bent slightly and neutrals go straight into a trap.
  6. Faraday cup: collects the charge to measure the dose.

Typical parameters (M4): ions P, As, Sb, B, In, O; dose 1011 to 1018 cm-2; energy 1 to 400 keV; uniformity ±1 %; flux 1012 to 1014 cm-2s-1; depths 10 nm to 10 μm.

Implant profile
C(x) = Cp exp[ −[[(x − Rp)²|2ΔRp²]] ]    Rp = projected range (mean depth), ΔRp = straggle (standard deviation)
Dose: Q = ∫C dx = √(2π) ΔRp Cp ⇒ Cp = Q/(√(2π)ΔRp) ≈ 0.4Q/ΔRp
{{fig:implant|Same dose, same energy: light boron goes deep and spreads out; heavy antimony stays shallow and narrow.}}
  • Stopping: ions lose energy by nuclear stopping (collisions with Si nuclei: large deflections, they knock atoms out of place, crystal damage) and electronic stopping (drag by electrons: path almost unchanged, small energy loss, negligible damage). Light ions lose energy mostly electronically ⇒ deep; heavy ions mostly by nuclear collisions ⇒ shallow and damaging.
  • Annealing is mandatory: a heat treatment that repairs the lattice damage and moves implanted dopants from interstitial to substitutional sites (activation).
  • Mask thickness: must be enough that the concentration that gets through is below the substrate background: d ≥ Rp + ΔRp√(2 ln(Cp/CB)) (with the range values for the mask material).
Worked: a 1015 cm-2 implant with Rp = 0.3 μm, ΔRp = 0.07 μm. Find the peak concentration. How thick must a mask (assume the same Rp, ΔRp) be so that less than 1015 cm-3 gets through?
  1. Cp = 1015/(2.507 × 7×10-6 cm) = 5.7×1019 cm-3.
  2. ln(5.7×1019/1015) = 10.95 ⇒ √(2 × 10.95) = 4.68.
  3. d = 0.3 + 0.07 × 4.68 = 0.63 μm.

14.8 Photolithography

The cycle (repeated for every layer): deposit the film → coat with photoresist (spin-coat for uniform thickness) → soft-bake → align the mask and expose with UV (stepper/scanner) → develop → etch or implant through the openings → strip the resist. History (Drive): Moore's 1965 observation of doubling every ~2 years at similar cost; the first commercial monolithic IC (1961) had 4 transistors; Nvidia Hopper (2022) has 80 billion.

{{fig:litho_resist|Positive vs negative resist, same mask.}}

Positive resist

Exposed areas become soluble and wash away ⇒ the pattern left is the same as the mask. Higher resolution, better dimension (CD) control and alignment. The industry standard.

Negative resist

Exposed areas cross-link and become insoluble; the unexposed areas wash away ⇒ the pattern is the inverse of the mask. Better etch resistance, thicker films, but lower resolution (it swells in the developer).

Rayleigh resolution
R = k1 [[λ|NA]]    NA = n sin θ (numerical aperture of the lens; θ = half-angle of the light cone)    k1 = process factor (≥ 0.25)
Smaller features need: ↓λ, ↑NA, ↓k1
  • ↓λ: mercury lamps (g-line 436 nm, i-line 365 nm; many lines need filters; lamp ageing needs recalibration; chromatic aberration) → excimer lasers: DUV 248 nm (KrF), 193 nm (ArF) → EUV 13.5 nm for 7/5/3 nm nodes. EUV light is made by hitting tin droplets (50,000 per second) with two infrared laser pulses (one flattens the droplet, the next vaporises it into a plasma). EUV is absorbed by everything, including glass and air, so it needs reflective multilayer mirrors in vacuum instead of lenses.
  • ↑NA: large aspheric lenses (beyond ~0.8), then immersion lithography: water between the lens and wafer raises n, so NA up to ~1.35. 193 nm immersion is the industry workhorse.
  • ↓k1: computational lithography and optical proximity correction (OPC) (pre-distort the mask so the printed shape comes out right; sub-resolution assist features; free-form OPC), off-axis (annular, dipole) illumination with programmable micromirror illuminators (FlexRay), phase-shift masks, and multiple patterning (split one dense layer into two or more exposures).
Worked: find the resolution of (a) 193 nm immersion with NA = 1.35 and k1 = 0.3, (b) EUV with NA = 0.33 and k1 = 0.4.

(a) R = 0.3 × 193/1.35 = 43 nm. (b) R = 0.4 × 13.5/0.33 = 16 nm. EUV gains mostly from the 14× shorter wavelength, even though its NA is lower.

14.9 Thin films and epitaxy (M6)

Films on a chip are dielectrics (SiO2, Si3N4), semiconductors (polysilicon, epitaxial Si), metals and polymers.

Epitaxy ("epi" = upon, "taxis" = ordered): growing a single-crystal film that continues the substrate's crystal.

  • Homoepitaxy: same material (Si on Si), to get a purer layer with different doping. Heteroepitaxy: different material (e.g. AlGaAs on GaAs), for band engineering.
  • Why: a high-purity device layer; growth at a lower temperature than diffusion, so doping steps stay sharp; free choice of thickness and doping; heterojunction bipolar transistors (Kroemer, Nobel Prize 2000); HEMTs with a 2-D electron gas; SOI, silicon-on-sapphire, SiC; a lightly doped p layer on a p⁺ substrate to reduce latch-up in CMOS; buried layers in bipolar ICs.
  • Types: VPE (vapour phase: a CVD process at 800 to 1100 °C), LPE (liquid phase, from a melt), MBE (molecular beam epitaxy: atomic beams in ultra-high vacuum ~10-8 Pa, monolayer control).
  • The seven CVD steps: (1) gas transport to the reactor, (2) diffusion across the boundary layer, (3) adsorption on the surface, (4) surface reaction, (5) desorption of by-products, (6) diffusion of by-products back across the boundary layer, (7) removal. Steps 2 to 5 set the growth rate; 3 to 5 are the surface reaction.
  • Reactors: horizontal atmospheric cold-wall (RF-heated graphite susceptor; SiCl4 + 2H2 → Si + 4HCl), low-pressure hot-wall (polysilicon, SiO2), pancake, barrel.
  • Si sources and temperatures: SiCl4 ~1200 °C, SiHCl3 ~1150 °C, SiH2Cl2 ~1100 °C, SiH4 ~1050 °C. The reverse reaction SiCl4 + Si ⇌ 2SiCl2 etches: growth rate vs SiCl4 mole fraction peaks near y ≈ 0.1 and becomes etching above y ≈ 0.27.

14.10 Putting it together: the metal-gate NMOS process

{{fig:nmos_flow|Key cross-sections of the NMOS flow in your Drive notes.}}
The 20 steps of slide M2 (four masks)
  1. Start with a p-type substrate.
  2. Grow a thick SiO2 layer (900 to 1200 °C).
  3. Coat with positive photoresist.
  4. Mask 1 (source/drain windows): lithography.
  5. UV changes the exposed resist.
  6. Developer removes the exposed resist.
  7. Etch the uncovered SiO2 with HF (hydrofluoric acid).
  8. Strip the remaining resist with piranha (H2SO4 : H2O2 ≈ 3 : 1).
  9. Form the n⁺ source and drain by diffusion or implantation.
  10. Cover the wafer with SiO2 again.
  11. Coat with positive resist.
  12. Mask 2: lithography for the gate region (and contact openings).
  13. Expose.
  14. Develop/remove the exposed resist.
  15. Etch the SiO2 over the gate region.
  16. Grow the thin gate oxide.
  17. Mask 3: remove the thin oxide over the source/drain contacts, keeping it over the gate.
  18. Deposit metal (Al) over everything.
  19. Mask 4: pattern the Al to separate source, gate and drain.
  20. Passivation / overglassing to protect the chip, leaving the bond pads open.

A CMOS process adds an n-well (Chapter 10 figure) for the pMOS, p⁺ implants for its source/drain, field-oxide isolation and, in modern processes, a polysilicon (or metal) self-aligned gate, followed by many layers of copper interconnect.

Exam traps
  • Oxide grows at the Si/SiO2 interface, consuming 0.44 of its thickness in Si; it does not just sit on top.
  • Thin oxide: linear, reaction-limited. Thick oxide: parabolic, diffusion-limited.
  • Pre-deposition = constant surface concentration (erfc). Drive-in = constant dose (Gaussian).
  • Implant: light ion deeper; energy sets depth; always needs an anneal.
  • Positive resist: exposed part dissolves, pattern = mask. Negative: the opposite.
  • R = k1λ/NA: immersion raises NA; EUV lowers λ.
  • CZ: crucible clockwise, seed anticlockwise; pull rate sets the diameter; O from the crucible, C from the susceptor.
