13.1 Crystals, Brillouin zone and band structure vs band diagram

  • Only a crystalline material (atoms repeating periodically in x, y, z) has a well-defined band gap. A completely full valence band carries no current, because there are no empty states for electrons to move into.
  • Example gaps from the Drive: metals have no gap (Au, Ag, Zn); an insulator ≈ 8 eV (Al2O3; SiO2 ≈ 9 eV); Si 1.1, Ge 0.7 eV. Room-temperature kT = 0.0259 eV.
  • E = ħ²k²/2m is exact only for a free electron in vacuum. In a crystal the periodic potential (solved with the Schrödinger equation) distorts the parabola and opens gaps at k = ±π/a, ±2π/a, … (Chapter 2 figure). The range −π/a ≤ k ≤ +π/a is the first Brillouin zone; everything else repeats it. Near k = 0 the band is still roughly parabolic, which is why the effective mass works.
  • Why m* matters (Drive): it packs all the quantum mechanics into one number so we can use plain F = ma for electrons in a crystal. Higher curvature ⇒ lower m* ⇒ higher mobility ⇒ higher current and higher speed.
  • Band structure (E vs k) shows allowed energies against momentum: used for direct/indirect gaps and m*. Band diagram (E vs x) shows Ec, Ev, EF against position in a device: used for junctions and MOS. Don't mix the two pictures.
  • Direct gap materials (GaAs, GaN) have a high absorption coefficient and emit light efficiently. Indirect ones (Si, Ge) need a phonon, so they absorb weakly and hardly emit; Si solar cells still work, they just need to be thicker.
  • Moore's law (Drive, file 1): transistor count per chip doubles about every two years while the cost per transistor falls.

13.2 Density of states and the carrier integrals

Your notes (set A p.8) wrote n = ∫ g(E) f(E) dE. The Drive gives the actual shapes:

{{fig:dos_carriers|Multiply the number of seats g(E) by the chance each is taken f(E): the shaded areas are n and p.}}
Density of states (states per eV per cm³)
gc(E) = [[4π(2mn*)3/2|h³]] √(E − Ec) for E ≥ Ec     gv(E) = [[4π(2mp*)3/2|h³]] √(Ev − E) for E ≤ Ev
Inside the gap g = 0, so there are no electrons there even where f = 0.5.

Doing the integral gives n = NcF1/2(η), where F1/2 is the Fermi–Dirac integral of order ½. If EF is at least 3kT away from the band edge, the Fermi function's tail is a plain exponential (the Boltzmann approximation) and the integral becomes simple:

Non-degenerate carrier concentrations
n = Nc exp[−(Ec − EF)/kT]     p = Nv exp[−(EF − Ev)/kT]
Effective densities of states: Nc = 2(2πmn*kT/h²)3/2, Nv = 2(2πmp*kT/h²)3/2; Si at 300 K: Nc ≈ 2.8×1019, Nv ≈ 1.04×1019 cm-3 (both ∝ T3/2)
Multiply: np = NcNve−Eg/kT = ni² ⇒ ni = √(NcNv) exp(−Eg/2kT) (this is why mass action holds)
Worked: (a) compute ni for Si. (b) Where is EF in Si with n = 1016 cm-3? Is the Boltzmann approximation valid?

(a) √(2.8×1019 × 1.04×1019) = 1.71×1019; Eg/2kT = 1.12/0.0518 = 21.6; e−21.6 = 4.1×10-10 ⇒ ni ≈ 7×109 cm-3. Textbooks quote 1×1010 or 1.5×1010 depending on the m* and Eg values used; use whatever the question gives.

(b) Ec − EF = kT ln(Nc/n) = 0.0259 × ln(2800) = 0.0259 × 7.94 = 0.206 eV below Ec. That is 8kT > 3kT ⇒ Boltzmann is fine.

The Drive stresses that ni cannot be reduced: it is the minimum carrier concentration that heat always creates. Higher ni means more leakage current and noise: Ge (0.68 eV) is leaky, GaN (3.4 eV) has a vanishingly small ni.

13.3 Temperature: freeze-out, extrinsic, intrinsic

{{fig:n_vs_T|Electron concentration of n-type Si against temperature (computed with incomplete ionisation and ni(T)).}}
  1. Freeze-out (≈ 10 to 50 K up to ~100 K): kT ≪ Ec − ED, so donors keep their electrons; n falls exponentially as T drops.
  2. Extrinsic (includes room temperature): donors ~100 % ionised, n ≈ ND, independent of T. This is the region devices are designed for.
  3. Intrinsic (very high T; for Si roughly 500 to 700 °C at moderate doping): ni ∝ e−Eg/2kT outgrows ND; the doping no longer controls the conductivity. Wide-gap GaN and SiC stay extrinsic to much higher temperatures, so they are used for hot, high-power electronics.

13.4 Heavy doping and incomplete ionisation

  • Degenerate semiconductor: at very high doping EF comes within 3kT (≈ 78 meV at 300 K) of the band edge or even enters the band (EF > Ec). The Boltzmann formula fails. The Drive gives the Joyce–Dixon approximation: (EF − Ec)/kT = ln(n/Nc) + (1/√8)(n/Nc), solved numerically.
  • Band-gap narrowing: heavy doping distorts the lattice potential so Eg shrinks (Si 1.1 → ~1.0 eV) and the band edges grow band tails.
  • Incomplete ionisation: a dopant is fully ionised only if its level is within about kT of the band. At 3 to 4 kT it is only partly ionised. Example from the Drive: Mg acceptors in GaN are only 1 to 5 % ionised (1017 Mg gives about 1015 holes), a real problem for blue/white LEDs.
Ionisation fractions
[[ND+|ND]] = [[1|1 + 2 exp[(EF − ED)/kT]]] (factor 2: spin)     [[NA−|NA]] = [[1|1 + 4 exp[(EA − EF)/kT]]] (factor 4: spin × heavy/light holes)
Charge neutrality must use the ionised numbers: p + ND+ = n + NA−.
Worked: what fraction of donors is ionised when EF = ED? When EF is 3kT below ED? When 2kT above?

EF = ED: 1/(1 + 2) = 33 % (the Drive's number). 3kT below: 1/(1 + 2e−3) = 91 %. 2kT above: 1/(1 + 2e2) = 6 %.

13.5 Scattering and mobility, the details

  • Scattering is not a billiard-ball hit: a disturbance in the periodic potential deflects the electron. Two sources: phonons (lattice vibrations; modes are acoustic and optical, each transverse and longitudinal), stronger at high T; and ionised impurities (Coulomb), stronger at low T and high doping.
  • The drift cycle: accelerate, gain energy, scatter, lose energy, repeat. Velocity = random thermal part (no current) + drift part (only with a field; the field can be internal, from a band slope).
  • μ = qτ/m* (cm²/V·s): mobility is the slope of v vs E, ∝ τ and ∝ 1/m* (holes are heavier, so slower).
  • Low field: v = μE. High field: v saturates, dv/dE = 0, and mobility no longer matters. Many short-channel transistors work in this regime (Chapter 11).
  • The Drive warns that this temperature dependence matters for design: CMOS logic timing can fail when mobility drops on a hot chip.
Worked: electrons in Si have μn = 1350 cm²/V·s and m* = 0.26m0. Find the mean time between collisions.

τ = μm*/q = (0.135 m²/V·s × 0.26 × 9.11×10-31 kg)/1.6×10-19 C = 2.0×10-13 s = 0.2 ps. (Convert μ to SI first: 1350 cm²/V·s = 0.135 m²/V·s.)

13.6 Einstein's relation: the derivation, and the built-in field of non-uniform doping

Your notes state Dn/μn = kT/q. The Drive derives it:

  1. Take an n-type bar in equilibrium whose doping falls from left to right. EF is flat (equilibrium), so Ec − EF grows to the right: the bands tilt.
  2. A tilted band means a field: E = (1/q) dEc/dx (as on your set-A p.23). This is the built-in field of non-uniform doping.
  3. n(x) = Nc exp[(EF − Ec(x))/kT] ⇒ dn/dx = −(n/kT) dEc/dx.
  4. In equilibrium the total current is zero, so drift balances diffusion in magnitude: qDn|dn/dx| = qμnnE ⇒ qDn(n/kT)(dEc/dx) = μnn(dEc/dx).
  5. Cancel: Dn/μn = kT/q. Although derived at equilibrium, it holds generally.

13.7 Traps and Shockley–Read–Hall statistics

Physical defects (dislocations, missing lines of atoms, vacancies, dangling bonds, impurities, interstitials) create discrete states at ET inside the gap. They matter in p–n junctions, BJTs, LEDs and solar cells. Four processes happen at a trap:

ProcessWhat happensRate (Drive)
raelectron capture (CB → empty trap)σnvthnNt(1 − f)
rbelectron emission (full trap → CB)enNtf
rchole capture (full trap's electron drops to VB)σpvthpNtf
rdhole emission (VB electron jumps up to the empty trap)epNt(1 − f)

σ = capture cross-section (cm²), vth = thermal velocity (≈ 107 cm/s), so σvth is the volume an electron sweeps per second; f = trap occupancy (a Fermi function at Et). In steady state ra − rb = rc − rd, and solving gives your SRH formula U = (pn − ni²)/[τ(p + n + 2ni)] for mid-gap traps, with

τ = [[1|σ vth Nt]]    more traps ⇒ shorter lifetime ⇒ faster recombination. (The Drive writes n for the density; physically it is the trap density Nt.)

Recombination can be radiative (energy leaves as light, band-to-band in direct-gap materials) or non-radiative (energy leaves as heat/phonons, typical of traps).

Worked: σ = 10-15 cm², vth = 107 cm/s, Nt = 1014 cm-3. Find τ. What happens if contamination raises Nt to 1016?

τ = 1/(10-15 × 107 × 1014) = 1 μs. At Nt = 1016: τ = 10 ns, 100× shorter. This is why fabs fight metal contamination.

13.8 Optical generation and the continuity equation

A photon can create an electron–hole pair only if its energy reaches the gap: hν = hc/λ ≥ Eg ⇒ λ ≤ hc/Eg (cut-off wavelength). Handy form: λ(μm) = 1.24/Eg(eV). Si: 1.24/1.12 = 1.1 μm, so Si detects visible and near-IR light. GL = optical generation rate (cm-3s-1).

The continuity equation is bookkeeping for a thin slice A·dx: (rate of change of carriers) = (flow in − flow out) + (generation) − (recombination):

Continuity equation (electrons)
[[∂n|∂t]] = [[1|q]] [[∂Jn|∂x]] + GL − U    (for minority electrons in p-type, U = Δn/τ)
Steady state, no field (diffusion only): Dn [[d²Δn|dx²]] + GL − [[Δn|τ]] = 0
With GL = 0: d²Δn/dx² = Δn/Ln²,   Ln = √(Dnτn)  ⇒  Δn(x) = A1ex/Ln + A2e−x/Ln
{{fig:continuity|The Drive's two examples: decay in time after the light is switched off, and decay in space away from where carriers are injected.}}
  • Example 1, uniform illumination (no gradient, steady): GL = Δn/τ ⇒ Δn = GLτ.
  • Example 2, light switched off at t = 0 (uniform): dΔn/dt = −Δn/τ ⇒ Δn(t) = GLτ e−t/τ. Watching this decay is how lifetime is measured.
  • Ln is the average distance an excess electron diffuses before it recombines; it is the Ln, Lp in your diode current (set A p.26).
Worked: p-type Si, μn = 1350 cm²/V·s, τn = 1 μs, uniformly lit with GL = 1020 cm-3s-1. Find Δn, Dn, Ln, and Δn 2 μs after the light goes off.
  1. Δn = GLτ = 1020 × 10-6 = 1014 cm-3.
  2. Dn = μnkT/q = 1350 × 0.0259 = 35 cm²/s.
  3. Ln = √(35 × 10-6) = 5.9×10-3 cm = 59 μm.
  4. After 2 μs = 2τ: Δn = 1014 e−2 = 1.35×1013 cm-3.

13.9 The p–n junction in equilibrium: derivations

Assumptions (Drive): moderate doping (no band tails or Joyce–Dixon), full ionisation, no light or external fields, and the depletion approximation (absolutely no mobile carriers inside W, perfectly neutral outside). The neutral regions have no field; EF is constant everywhere, which forces the bands to bend.

Explain like I'm 10: the "cemented floor" (Drive)

Imagine Ec is a floor made of cement. Electrons are marbles that roll downhill on it. Across the junction the floor slopes down toward the n-side, so marbles that wander to the p-side roll back. The slope is the built-in field (pointing n → p), and its height is qVbi. Holes are bubbles that float up the Ev floor, so they are pushed back to the p-side.

Built-in voltage, the Drive's derivation
n-side: Ec − EF = kT ln(Nc/ND)    p-side: EF − Ev = kT ln(Nv/NA)
qVbi = Eg − (Ec − EF)n − (EF − Ev)p = Eg − kT ln[NcNv/(NAND)]
Use ni² = NcNve−Eg/kT ⇒ ln(NcNv) = ln ni² + Eg/kT; the Eg terms cancel ⇒ Vbi = (kT/q) ln(NAND/ni²) (your set-A p.21 formula)
More doping or a wider gap (smaller ni) ⇒ larger Vbi; maximum ≈ Eg/q at degenerate doping. Vbi cannot be measured with a voltmeter (contact potentials cancel it).
Poisson's equation and the field (n-side, 0 ≤ x ≤ Wn)
d²V/dx² = −ρ/εs with ρ = qND ⇒ field is a straight line: |E(x)| = [[qND|εs]](Wn − x), zero at the depletion edge

Potential drops: Vn = qNDWn²/2εs, Vp = qNAWp²/2εs, Vbi = Vn + Vp = ½|Emax|W ⇒ the W formula of your set-A p.22
Worked: Si p⁺n junction, NA = 1017, ND = 1015 cm-3, ni = 1.5×1010. Find Vbi, W, Wn, Wp, Emax, and W at 5 V reverse bias.
  1. Vbi = 0.0259 ln(1032/2.25×1020) = 0.0259 × 26.82 = 0.695 V.
  2. W = √[(2 × 1.04×10-12 × 0.695/1.6×10-19)(10-17 + 10-15)] = √(9.13×10-9) = 0.955 μm.
  3. Wn = W·NA/(NA + ND) = 0.946 μm; Wp = 9.5 nm: the depletion lies almost entirely in the lightly doped side.
  4. Emax = 2Vbi/W = 1.39/9.55×10-5 = 1.45×104 V/cm.
  5. At VR = 5 V replace Vbi by Vbi + VR = 5.695 V: W = 0.955 × √(5.695/0.695) = 2.73 μm.

Very high doping on both sides (≈ 1018 and above) makes W only 10 to 20 nm, thin enough for electrons to tunnel straight through: the tunnel diode.

13.10 The biased junction: quasi-Fermi levels, long and short diodes

  • Under bias there is no single EF. Each carrier type gets its own quasi-Fermi level, EFn and EFp. Their separation across the junction equals qVa; they merge into one EF at Va = 0. In general np = ni² e(EFn − EFp)/kT, which is where your pn(xn) = pn0eV/VT comes from.
  • Barrier under bias: q(Vbi − Va). Drive example: 0.76 − 0.4 = 0.36 V forward; 0.76 + 2 = 2.76 V at 2 V reverse.
  • Long diode (neutral region ≫ Lp): excess carriers decay exponentially, as in your notes. Short diode (neutral region ≪ Lp): almost no recombination, so the profile is a straight line and Lp is replaced by the region length in J0. The BJT base is a short diode.
  • Total current is the same at every x (Kirchhoff). Far from the junction the minority diffusion current has died away, so the current is carried by majority-carrier drift in the quasi-neutral regions. That's why your notes (p.27) add the drift picture.
  • J0 ∝ ni²: Ge leaks a lot, GaN very little, so wide-gap diodes are better rectifiers (power devices, low dark current in photodetectors). In reverse bias the minority concentration at the depletion edge is ≈ 0; the tiny J0 is made of thermally generated minority carriers that wander to the edge and are swept across.

13.11 Generation–recombination currents and the ideality factor

The ideal-diode law assumed no recombination inside the depletion region. Real junctions contain traps there, where p and n are both small, which is exactly where SRH is most effective. So Jtotal = Jideal + JGR. Sign of U: U > 0 = net recombination (forward bias); U < 0 = net generation (reverse bias).

Recombination current (forward)
Assume mid-gap traps and p ≈ n at the worst point; with pn = ni²eqVa/kT ⇒ p ≈ n ≈ nieqVa/2kT
Umax ≈ [[ni²eqV/kT|τ · 2nieqV/2kT]] = [[ni|2τ]] eqV/2kT  ⇒  Jrec ≈ [[qniW|2τ]] eqVa/2kT
Generation current (reverse)
p, n ≪ ni ⇒ U ≈ −ni/2τ (net generation from traps, not light) ⇒ Jgen = [[qniW|2τ]]    Jrev = −(J0 + Jgen)
Jgen ∝ W ∝ √(Vbi + VR), so real reverse current creeps up with VR until breakdown.
Ideality factor η

13.12 Breakdown, in depth

  • Breakdown happens only in reverse, where almost the whole voltage drops across the depletion region. It is not destructive unless the current is allowed to grow without limit (Zener diodes work there on purpose).
  • Zener (tunnelling): both sides heavily doped ⇒ W very thin ⇒ VB electrons on the p-side tunnel straight into empty CB states on the n-side through a thin triangular barrier. VBR ≲ 4Eg/q (≈ 4.4 V for Si; your notes: < 5 V).
  • Avalanche (impact ionisation): lighter doping, wider W; a carrier gains enough energy to knock a new electron–hole pair out of the lattice, and the new carriers do the same: a chain reaction. VBR ≳ 6Eg/q (> 6.6 V for Si; your notes: > 8 V). The most common mechanism.
  • Impact-ionisation coefficient αn = (1/n)(dn/dx) (new pairs per cm). Multiplication factor M = 1/(1 − ∫0Wα dx); breakdown when the integral reaches 1 (M → ∞).
  • Critical field Fcrit: Si ≈ 4×105 V/cm, GaN ≈ 3×106 V/cm, weakly dependent on doping. The field is triangular, so VBR = ½ W Fmax. For W = 1 μm: Si ½ × 10-4 × 4×105 = 20 V; GaN ≈ 150 V (the Drive quotes 180 V with a slightly larger Fcrit). That is why GaN and SiC are used for high-power switches.
Worked: one-sided Si junction with ND = 1016 cm-3 on the light side. Estimate the avalanche breakdown voltage.

At breakdown Emax = qNDW/εs = Fcrit and VBR = ½WFcrit ⇒ VBR = εsFcrit²/(2qND) = 1.04×10-12 × (4×105)²/(2 × 1.6×10-19 × 1016) = 52 V. Lighter doping ⇒ higher breakdown voltage.

13.15 The MOS capacitor in band-diagram language

{{fig:mos_bands|p-type MOS capacitor: bands bend up in accumulation and down in depletion; strong inversion is when the surface band bending ψs reaches 2φF.}}
  • Ideal flat band: metal and semiconductor work functions equal (φm = φs), so at VG = 0 the Fermi levels line up and the bands are flat. A gate voltage separates the two Fermi levels by qVG.
  • Bulk potential φF (ψBP): qφF = Ei − EF in the bulk, a measure of how p-type the substrate is: φF = VT ln(NA/ni).
  • Accumulation (VG < 0): holes pile up, bands bend up at the surface (Ev moves toward EF).
  • Depletion (small VG > 0): holes pushed away, bands bend down; the surface becomes less p-type.
  • Weak inversion starts when Ei at the surface reaches EF (band bending = φF: surface is intrinsic).
  • Strong inversion: Ei at the surface is φF below EF, so the surface is as n-type as the bulk is p-type. Total band bending ψs = 2φF. This is the threshold condition used in Chapter 9.
  • Flat-band voltage VFB: in a real device (φm ≠ φs, charge in the oxide) the bands are not flat at zero bias; VFB is the gate voltage that flattens them. Vth = VFB + 2φF + Qd/Cox.
The C–V curve, all four cases (MOS Capacitor_2)
  • C is measured with a small AC signal on top of the DC bias: C = ΔQ/ΔV.
  • Accumulation: majority holes respond instantly at the interface ⇒ C = Cox (the maximum).
  • Depletion: Cdep = εs/WD in series with Cox; VG ↑ ⇒ WD ↑ ⇒ C ↓.
  • Strong inversion: WD stops at WD,max (the inversion layer screens the field) ⇒ Cmin. Low frequency (~100 Hz): minority electrons have time to be generated and follow the signal ⇒ C climbs back to Cox. High frequency (kHz to MHz): they can't follow; only holes at the edge of Wmax respond ⇒ C stays at Cmin.
  • Deep depletion: if the DC bias is swept very fast, no inversion layer has time to form; W keeps growing past Wmax and C falls below Cmin.
  • At flat band the capacitance is not Cox: the AC signal still moves charge to a small depth, the Debye length LD = √(εskT/(q²NA)). CDebye = εs/LD, and CFB = Cox in series with CDebye.
Worked: NA = 1016 cm-3, tox = 10 nm. Find LD, CDebye and CFB/Cox.
  1. LD = √(εsVT/(qNA)) = √(1.04×10-12 × 0.0259/(1.6×10-19 × 1016)) = 4.1×10-6 cm = 41 nm.
  2. CDebye = 1.04×10-12/4.1×10-6 = 2.53×10-7 F/cm²; Cox = 3.45×10-13/10-6 = 3.45×10-7 F/cm².
  3. CFB = 1/(1/3.45 + 1/2.53) × 10-7 = 1.46×10-7 ⇒ CFB/Cox ≈ 0.42.

13.16 The BJT, quantitatively (BJT1, BJT2)

  • Applications (Drive): switching in power electronics (block 1000 V or pass 10 A from a tiny control signal; DC–DC converters, inverters, motor drives, EV chargers), logic, memory, and amplifiers (power amplifiers, low-noise amplifiers at GHz).
  • Four steps in an npn: injection (E → B), transport and recombination in the base, collection (the reverse-biased CB field sweeps electrons into C), control: IC is set by VBE (injection) and base transport, not by VC.
  • Control mechanism: a small change in IB changes VBE a little, and because IE is exponential in VBE, IC changes a lot: amplification.
  • Base current IB = IEp (holes back-injected into the emitter) + IB,rec (holes lost to recombination in the base). IC = Inc + IBC,rev (pA, neglected).

γ ≈ 1 − [[NABDeW|NDEDnLe]]    B = [[1|cosh(W/Ln)]] ≈ 1 − [[W²|2Ln²]]    α = Bγ    β = α/(1 − α)
β limited by back-injection: β ≈ [[DnLeNDE|DeWNAB]]    β limited by base recombination: β ≈ [[2Ln²|W²]]
Maximise β: raise NDE, lower NAB, shrink W (your set-A p.35 conclusions, now as formulas)
Worked: W = 1 μm, Ln = 10 μm, Le = 1 μm, Dn = 20, De = 2 cm²/s, NDE = 1019, NAB = 1017 cm-3. Find B, γ, α, β.
  1. B = 1/cosh(0.1) = 0.995 (recombination-limited β alone would be 2 × 100 = 200).
  2. γ = 1 − (1017 × 2 × 1)/(1019 × 20 × 1) = 1 − 0.001 = 0.999 (injection-limited β alone would be 1000).
  3. α = 0.995 × 0.999 = 0.994 ⇒ β = 0.994/0.006 ≈ 166. Base recombination is the main limit here; a thinner base would help most.
  • CB configuration: input IE, output IC, gain α < 1 (5 mA in, 4.9 mA out); output curves IC vs VCB, flat, stepping with IE. CE configuration: input IB (μA), output IC (mA), gain β (5 μA in, 0.5 to 5 mA out); curves IC vs VCE.
  • Early effect and its trade-off: the base is lightly doped, so the CB depletion region spreads into it as VCB rises, WBn falls and IC rises (sloped curves meeting at −VA; ideal VA = ∞). The Gummel number QG ≈ NABWBn (majority charge in the base) sets it: VA ∝ QG/CBC. A heavily doped base gives a big VA but lowers γ and β: the β vs VA trade-off. To raise VA without hurting β, make CBC small, i.e. a wide BC depletion region: the collector is the most lightly doped region.
Exam traps
  • Boltzmann formulas need EF at least 3kT from the band edge; otherwise the material is degenerate.
  • Donor factor 2, acceptor factor 4 in the ionisation formulas.
  • λcut-off = 1.24/Eg μm: a larger gap means a shorter wavelength.
  • Recombination current has η = 2 (eqV/2kT), diffusion η = 1.
  • Zener needs heavy doping on both sides; avalanche happens in lightly doped junctions and has the higher VBR.
  • High-frequency C–V stays at Cmin in inversion; low-frequency returns to Cox; deep depletion goes below Cmin.
  • BJT doping order: emitter heaviest, base medium/light, collector lightest.
