A transistor is a valve for electricity: a small control signal governs a big flow.

8.1 What are transistors used for, and what kinds exist?

Applications (your list): 1. switch, 2. logic gates, 3. memory devices, 4. amplifiers. Two big families:

1. Field-effect transistor (FET)

An electric field applied at the control terminal C controls the current between two terminals A and B. Almost no current enters the control terminal.

Examples: MOSFET, MESFET, MISFET (Chapters 9 and 10).

2. Junction transistor

A small amount of current injected at the control terminal C controls the (much larger) current between A and B.

Examples: BJT, hetero-junction BJT (HBT).

Explain like I'm 10

A garden tap. In a BJT you push a small amount of water into the tap's handle and that lets a big river flow. In a FET you don't push any water; you just bring an invisible magnet close to the handle and it opens up. BJT: "current controls current". FET: "voltage (field) controls current".

8.2 BJT structure

BJT = Bipolar Junction Transistor. "Bipolar" because both electrons and holes take part in conduction. It is a three-layer sandwich with two p–n junctions back-to-back:

  • Emitter (E): emits (injects) carriers. Heavily doped.
  • Base (B): the very thin, lightly doped middle layer that controls the flow.
  • Collector (C): collects the carriers. Moderately doped, physically the largest.
  • Junctions: EBJ = emitter–base junction, CBJ = collector–base junction.
  • Two flavours: npn (n–p–n; electrons do the work) and pnp (p–n–p; holes do the work). Your notes draw the pnp first and then analyse the npn, the more common type.

8.3 Band diagram of an npn transistor

Read it exactly as you read the diode: EF is flat (equilibrium, no current), the bands bend at each junction (depletion regions), and the p-type base has its bands raised relative to the n-type emitter and collector. Electrons in the emitter must climb the EBJ barrier to get into the base, and electrons in the base slide down the CBJ slope into the collector.

8.6 What happens inside an npn in active mode

Voltages: VBE forward-biases the EBJ, VCB reverse-biases the CBJ. Follow the carriers (your circled numbers ①–⑤):

  1. Emitter → base. The EBJ is forward, so electrons flow from the heavily-doped n-emitter into the p-base. This is the electron current IEn ①. Holes also flow the other way, from base into emitter: IEp ②. Total emitter current: IE = IEn + IEp. The hole part is not useful (it doesn't reach the collector).
  2. Through the base. The base is very thin, so almost all the injected electrons (now minority carriers in the base) diffuse across without recombining. A few (1–2 %) recombine with holes in the base. Those lost holes must be replaced, and that replacement is the base current IB,rec ③ that we supply.
  3. Collector sweep. The reverse-biased CBJ has a strong field that sweeps the electrons arriving at the base edge into the collector: the useful collector current ICn ④.
  4. Leakage. The reverse-biased CBJ also carries a tiny leakage current IBC,rev ⑤ (minority carriers, like the diode's Is). It is usually neglected but exists.
Your notes say

"IC will increase if we increase the electrons going from emitter to base." "IEp is not used for output current." "Due to recombination of electrons and holes in the p-region, holes are reduced, therefore we provide IB,rec (base current)."

Explain like I'm 10

Kids (electrons) run from a huge crowded playground (emitter) across a very narrow bridge (base) into a big slide (collector) that carries them away. On the narrow bridge a couple of kids trip and get stuck (recombine); the teacher (base current) must send in a few replacements. The tiny number the teacher sends controls the huge number of kids flowing. That is amplification.

Current relations
IE = IC + IB    IC ≈ ICn    IB = IB,rec + IEp (+ leakage)
Beyond your notes

IC = IS eVBE/VT (exponential, like the diode) and IB = IC/β. So VBE ≈ 0.7 V is the usual "on" voltage for silicon.

Explain like I'm 10

Out of 100 kids leaving the playground, γ tells you how many head the right way (the rest run backwards and are wasted), and B tells you how many of the right-way kids make it across the bridge without tripping. Multiply the two and you get α, the fraction that reaches the slide. Say α = 0.98: 98 arrive and 2 are the ones the teacher must replace, so the gain is β = 98/2 = 49.

Notice β is extremely sensitive to α: α = 0.98 ⇒ β = 49; α = 0.99 ⇒ β = 99; α = 0.995 ⇒ β = 199. Improving efficiency by half a percent doubles the gain.

8.8 How to design a good BJT: base width and doping

Your notes say
  1. Width of base should be small to reduce recombination. Wbase ↓ ⇒ recombination ↓ ⇒ collector current ↑ (and B → 1).
  2. Currents from the diode formulas: IEn = q (Dn/Ln)(ni²/NA)(eV/VT − 1) [base doping NAB] and IEp = q (Dp/Lp)(ni²/ND)(eV/VT − 1) [emitter doping NDE], with the same applied VBE. To make IEn ≫ IEp we need NDE ≫ NAB.
  3. Therefore base: lightly doped, emitter: heavily doped.
RegionDopingWidthReason
Emitterheavy (n⁺)mediuminject lots of electrons; IEp is small ⇒ γE ≈ 1
Baselight (p)very thinfew holes to recombine with ⇒ B ≈ 1; thin so electrons cross before recombining
Collectormoderatelargestcollects and dissipates power; lightly enough doped to withstand high reverse voltage
Worked: emitter NDE = 1019 cm-3, base NAB = 1017 cm-3. Assume Dp/Dn = 0.4 and equal diffusion lengths. Find γE. If B = 0.995, find α and β.
  1. IEp/IEn = (Dp/Dn)(Ln/Lp)(NAB/NDE) = 0.4 × 1 × 1017/1019 = 0.004.
  2. γE = 1/(1 + 0.004) = 0.996.
  3. α = B × γE = 0.995 × 0.996 = 0.991.
  4. β = α/(1 − α) = 0.991/0.009 ≈ 110.

If someone lowered the emitter doping to 1017 (equal to the base), the ratio would be 0.4, γE = 0.71, and β would collapse to about 2.4. That's why the emitter must be heavily doped.

Worked: a BJT has IC = 2 mA and β = 100. Find IB, IE, α. Then the same transistor is replaced by one with β = 200: what changes if IB is kept fixed?

IB = IC/β = 2 mA/100 = 20 μA. IE = IC + IB = 2.02 mA. α = IC/IE = 2/2.02 = 0.990 (also β/(β+1) = 100/101 ✔). With IB fixed at 20 μA and β = 200: IC = 200 × 20 μA = 4 mA; IE = 4.02 mA; α = 0.995. The collector current doubles because β is a property of the device: a real design problem (β varies ±50 % between parts) that engineers fix with feedback.

8.9 Metallurgical vs neutral base width

  • Metallurgical base width = the physical (initial) distance between the two metallurgical junctions.
  • Neutral base width = the part of the base that is not depleted: the region where recombination happens and carriers diffuse. It is smaller than the metallurgical width because each depletion region eats into the base.
Wneutral = Wmetallurgical − (depletion penetration into the base from EBJ) − (depletion penetration into the base from CBJ)

8.10 Base-width modulation and the Early voltage

{{fig:bjt_curves|Your page 36. Ideal output curves are flat; real ones tilt upward. Extending the tilted lines backwards, they all meet the voltage axis at −V_A.}}
Explain like I'm 10

Imagine the narrow bridge (the base) has walls that can slowly squeeze it. When you increase the collector voltage, the depletion region at the collector junction grows and eats into the base, so the bridge gets even shorter. A shorter bridge means fewer kids trip on it, so more of them reach the slide and the collector current creeps up. This slow rise of IC with voltage is the Early effect (base-width modulation).

  • Ideal: in the active region IC is independent of VCB (or VCE): horizontal lines, like a perfect current source.
  • Practical: the neutral base width shrinks as VCB rises ⇒ recombination ↓ and the base gradient ↑ ⇒ IC ↑. The lines have a positive slope.
  • Extrapolate those sloping lines back to IC = 0: they all meet the voltage axis at the same point −VA. VA is the Early voltage (typically 50 to 200 V).
Beyond your notes: the formula
IC = IS eVBE/VT ( 1 + [[ VCE | VA ]] )    output resistance ro = [[ VA + VCE | IC ]] ≈ [[ VA | IC ]]

A larger VA means flatter lines and a better current source. Example: VA = 100 V, IC = 1 mA ⇒ ro ≈ 100 kΩ.

Exam traps for the BJT
  • Active mode means EBJ forward, CBJ reverse. Students often say "both forward".
  • The base current is not the input carriers of the collector current; it is only the small amount that replaces recombined carriers (plus the wasted hole current).
  • α is always < 1; β is always ≫ 1. β = α/(1−α), not α/(1+α).
  • Emitter arrow: it points in the direction of conventional current in the emitter: out of the emitter for npn ("Not Pointing iN"), into the emitter for pnp.
