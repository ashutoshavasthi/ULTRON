CHAPTER 4Doping: Seasoning the Crystal

Pure silicon is a poor conductor. Add a pinch of the right impurity and the conductivity jumps by a factor of a million.

Your notes p.9

4.1 Intrinsic (pure) vs extrinsic (doped)

  • Intrinsic semiconductor: perfectly pure. Every free electron in the CB came from a broken bond and left a hole behind, so n = p = ni. For Si at 300 K, ni ≈ 1.5×1010 cm-3 (some textbooks and your notes' questions use 1010). Silicon has 5×1022 atoms/cm3, so only 1 atom in 3 trillion has given a free electron.
  • Extrinsic semiconductor: impurity atoms deliberately added ("doping").
{{fig:doping|Your page 9: the lattice with one dopant. Phosphorus (5 valence electrons) has one electron too many; boron (3 valence electrons) is one short.}}

4.2 n-type doping: pentavalent atoms (donors)

Explain like I'm 10

Silicon atoms hold hands with four neighbours, using four electrons each. Now replace one silicon by phosphorus, which arrives with five hand-holders. Four form the bonds; the fifth is a spare with nobody to hold hands with, so it wanders off easily and can carry electricity. The phosphorus is now missing one electron, so it becomes a stuck-in-place positive ion (P+). It can't move, because it's locked in the crystal.

  • Dopants: phosphorus (P), arsenic (As), antimony (Sb). They are called donors because they donate an electron.
  • Donor concentration: ND (atoms per cm3). Each ionised donor becomes a fixed positive charge ND+.
  • Energy picture: the spare electron sits at a donor level Ed just below Ec (about 45 meV for P in Si). At room temperature (kBT = 26 meV) nearly all of them get kicked into the CB.
  • Result: n ≫ p. Electrons are the majority carriers, holes the minority carriers. EF moves up toward Ec.

4.3 p-type doping: trivalent atoms (acceptors)

Explain like I'm 10

Replace one silicon by boron, which brings only three hand-holders. One bond is left with an empty hand, a hole. A neighbouring electron happily jumps in to fill it, which leaves an empty hand behind at the neighbour, and so the hole travels around. The boron has accepted an extra electron, so it becomes a fixed negative ion (B−).

  • Dopants: boron (B), aluminium (Al), gallium (Ga), indium (In). Called acceptors.
  • Concentration NA; ionised acceptors are fixed negative charges NA−.
  • Energy picture: the acceptor level Ea lies just above Ev. A VB electron easily hops onto it, leaving a hole in the VB.
  • Result: p ≫ n. Holes are the majority carriers. EF moves down toward Ev.

Exam traps
  • n-type and p-type crystals are electrically neutral overall. "n" refers to the majority carrier being negative, not the crystal being charged.
  • The ion is immobile; only the electron or hole moves. This is what builds the depletion region later.
  • Doping adds carriers of one sign but the minority carrier count goes down (mass-action law, next chapter).
Beyond your notes: compensation

If both donors and acceptors are present, they cancel. The net doping is |ND − NA|, and the crystal is n-type if ND > NA, p-type otherwise. This is used in real fabrication (e.g. making an n-well inside a p-substrate).

CHAPTER 5Carrier Concentration: How Many Electrons and Holes?

Two simple laws, mass-action and charge neutrality, give you n and p for any doping.

Your notes p.10 to p.11

5.1 Mass-action law

Your notes say

At equilibrium, the product of electron and hole concentrations in a semiconductor is constant at a given temperature and independent of doping.

Mass-action law
n · p = ni²   (intrinsic: n = p = ni)
Explain like I'm 10

Imagine a see-saw with electrons on one side and holes on the other, and a rule that the two counts multiplied together always give the same number. If you pour 1,000 times more electrons in (n-type doping), holes must shrink by 1,000 times. More of one means proportionally less of the other. The reason: extra electrons bump into holes and annihilate them (recombination) until balance is restored.

Only two things change ni: the material (bigger Eg ⇒ smaller ni) and the temperature (hotter ⇒ much larger ni):

ni ∝ T3/2 exp( − Eg / 2kBT )

You will need this exact dependence for your assignment question (Si vs Ge diodes, Chapter 7).

5.2 Charge neutrality

A doped crystal is not charged overall. The positive charges must exactly balance the negative charges:

Charge neutrality
positive: holes p + ionised donors ND+    negative: electrons n + ionised acceptors NA−
p + ND = n + NA (assuming full ionisation)

5.3 The general solution (your derivation)

  1. Rewrite neutrality: n + (NA − ND) − p = 0.
  2. Multiply by n: n² + n(NA − ND) − np = 0.
  3. Use np = ni²: n² + (NA − ND) n − ni² = 0, a quadratic in n.
  4. Quadratic formula (take the + root because n must be positive):
n = [[ −(NA − ND) + √( (NA − ND)² + 4ni² ) | 2 ]]    then p = ni² / n

Equivalent and easier to remember: with net donor density (ND − NA),

n = [[ (ND − NA) + √( (ND − NA)² + 4ni² ) | 2 ]]

5.4 The two shortcuts you actually use

Case I: strongly n-type (ND ≫ NA)

n ≈ ND
p = ni² / ND

Case II: strongly p-type (NA ≫ ND)

p ≈ NA
n = ni² / NA

When are they valid? When the net doping is more than about 10× ni. Doping of 1015 to 1018 cm-3 in Si at room temperature (ni ≈ 1010) is far beyond that, so the shortcuts are always fine there. Use the full quadratic only when the net doping is comparable to ni (very light doping or very high temperature).

Worked: silicon at 300 K, ni = 1.5×1010 cm-3, doped with ND = 1016 cm-3 and nothing else. Find n, p and EF − Ei.
  1. ND ≫ ni, so n ≈ 1016 cm-3.
  2. p = ni²/n = (2.25×1020)/1016 = 2.25×104 cm-3. One hole for every 4.4×1011 electrons.
  3. EF − Ei = kBT ln(n/ni) = 0.02585 × ln(6.67×105) = 0.02585 × 13.41 = 0.347 eV above the mid-gap, so EF is close to Ec, as expected for n-type.
Worked: compensated sample, ND = 5×1016, NA = 2×1016 cm-3, ni = 1.5×1010. Find n and p.

Net = ND − NA = 3×1016 ≫ ni. So the sample is n-type with n ≈ 3×1016 and p = 2.25×1020/3×1016 = 7.5×103 cm-3.

Worked (when the shortcut fails): ni = 1010, net doping ND − NA = 1010 cm-3. Find n and p.

Doping is equal to ni, so use the full formula: n = [1010 + √(1020 + 4×1020)]/2 = [1010 + 2.236×1010]/2 = 1.618×1010 cm-3. Then p = 1020/1.618×1010 = 6.18×109 cm-3. Notice the shortcut (n = 1010) would be 38 % wrong here.

Extra: p-type Si with NA = 1017, ni = 1010. If the temperature rises until ni = 1017, what happens?

At that temperature ni equals the doping: the thermally generated carriers match the dopant-provided ones. The material stops behaving like p-type and starts behaving like intrinsic (n ≈ p). Full formula: p = [1017 + √(1034 + 4×1034)]/2 = 1.618×1017 and n = 6.18×1016. This is why silicon devices fail at very high temperature. A wide-gap material (GaN, SiC) reaches this point much later.

Exam traps
  • The mass-action product n·p = ni² only holds at equilibrium. Under light or injection, np > ni² (Chapter 6).
  • In the quadratic, take the + root. The − root gives negative n.
  • Units: concentrations are per cm3 in every formula in your notes, and mobilities are cm²/V·s. Mixing m3 and cm3 gives errors of 106.
