Light sources and lenses
Traditionally Hg vapor lamps have been used which generate many spectral lines from a high intensity plasma inside a glass lamp. 

Electrons are excited to higher energy levels by collisions in the plasma, and photons are emitted when the energy is released. 

Filters can be used to limit exposure wavelengths. 

Intensity uniformity has to be better than several % over the collection area. 

Needs spectral exposure meter for routine calibration due to aging.

--- slide 11 ---
Extreme UV Light Sources (EUV)
UV lithography uses extremely short-wavelength light (13.5 nm) for advanced nodes (7 nm, 5 nm, 3 nm).

Tiny molten tin droplets are fired into a vacuum 50,000 times per second.

Each droplet is hit by two infrared laser pulses:
1st pulse flattens the droplet.
2nd pulse vaporizes it into superheated tin plasma.

The tin plasma emits EUV radiation at 13.5 nm.

A large collector mirror captures and reflects the EUV light toward the scanner optics.

EUV light cannot pass through lenses, so the system uses only multilayer reflective mirrors in a vacuum.

EUV sources are essential for printing ultra-fine features at modern technology nodes.
 
--- slide 12 ---
Lenses and Numerical aperture
NA controls how much light the lens collects → determines resolution.

NA = n sin θ, n is the refractive index and θ is half of the maximum cone angle of light that the lens can accept.

Spherical lenses limited NA to ~0.8.

Aspherical lenses enabled NA > 0.8.

The physics of diffusion
Fick’s First Law of diffusion:

1. Flux is proportional to the concentration gradient.
2. The proportionality constant is the diffusivity (D), or diffusion coefficient. Units of D are cm2/sec. 
--- slide 16 ---
The physics of diffusion
Fick’s second law of diffusion:
It describes how concentration changes over time (dC/dt).


If more dopants flow into a small region than flow out, concentration increases.

Limitations of Fick’s law
Fick’s Laws work well for low doping concentrations.

However, they do not accurately describe diffusion at high doping concentrations or when other processes (like oxidation) are happening simultaneously.

For these complex scenarios, a deeper, atomistic view of diffusion physics is necessary.

Typical Ion Implantation Parameters
Ions: P, As, Sb, B, In, O
Dose: 10¹¹ – 10¹⁸ cm⁻²
Ion energy: 1 – 400 keV
Uniformity and reproducibility: ± 1%
Ion flux: 10¹² – 10¹⁴ cm⁻² s⁻¹
Ion distribution depths: 10 nm – 10 µm
A process in which a gas is first ionized to create charged ions, which are then accelerated by a high electric field and injected into the target semiconductor wafer to depths ranging from a few nanometers to several hundred nanometers.

--- slide 25 ---
Ion stopping mechanism
Ions penetrate into substrate
Collide with lattice atoms
Gradually lose their energy and stop

Nuclear Stopping
Collision with nuclei of the lattice atoms
Scattered significantly
Causes crystal structure damage

Electronic Stopping
Collision with electrons of the lattice atoms
Incident ion path is almost unchanged
Energy transfer is very small
Crystal structure damage is negligible
