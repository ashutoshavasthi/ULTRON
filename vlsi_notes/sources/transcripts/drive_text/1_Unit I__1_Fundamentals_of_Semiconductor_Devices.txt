Fundamentals of Semiconductor Devices 
I. Properties of Semiconductors 
A. Definition 
A semiconductor is a material whose conductivity lies between that of a metal (conductor) and an insulator 
(such as wood or glass). 
B. The Unique Property: Tunable Conductivity 
The key property of semiconductors is that their conductivity can be tuned or tailored by many orders of 
magnitude. 
• Unlike metals, where conductivity is difficult to change (except by raising temperature). 
• Unlike insulators (like glass), which will not conduct electricity no matter what is done. 
• This tunability allows devices to be made that cater to specific needs. 
C. Role as a Switch and Amplifier 
Semiconductors possess the unique capability to function both as a switch and an amplifier. 
• Switching Action: They can allow a very high current to flow (like a conductor in the ON state) AND can 
block very large voltages (like an insulator in the OFF state). 
• This ability to block voltage and switch states is not found in metals. 
• Amplification: They can be used as an amplifier, which enlarges the signal and provides gain, useful in 
devices like RF devices.

The transistor is one of the most fundamental building blocks of all electronic circuits. A transistor is a device 
where the current flowing between two terminals (A and B) is controlled not by the voltage applied between 
A and B, but by the voltage applied at a third control point (C). 
The complexity and functionality of devices have increased immensely due to the shrinking size of transistors.

Moore's Law: The number of transistors in a chip doubles approximately every two years, leading to 
exponential increases in computing power and a decrease in cost per transistor. This is achieved because 
transistors are constantly becoming smaller (shrinking sizes), allowing more components to be 
accommodated in the same chip area

IV. Energy Bands and the Band Diagram 
Energy bands are ranges of allowed energy levels that electrons in a solid can occupy due to the overlap of 
atomic orbitals when atoms are brought close together to form a crystal. 
• 
Valence Band (VB): The highest range of energies where electrons are normally present at 0 K.  
• 
Conduction Band (CB): The range of energies higher than the valence band where electrons can move 
freely and contribute to electrical conduction. 
• 
Band Gap (Eg): The forbidden region (no allowed energy states) between the conduction band and 
the valence band. 
A. Formation of Energy Bands 
 
The energy bands arise from the interaction of atoms when they are closely packed in a crystal. 
• Isolated atom: For a single, isolated atom, electrons exist in discrete energy levels or shells (like 1s, 2s, 2p) 
• Atoms brought closer:  In a real crystal, atoms are separated by a fixed spacing called the lattice constant. 
When isolated atoms are brought close together (distance r decreases) towards this equilibrium spacing (req), 
their valence orbitals start to interact. 
• Merging & splitting: The discrete energy levels interact with each other and split into two new bands, the 
valence band and the conduction band.   
• Result: At the equilibrium spacing, the electrons fully occupy the lower band (the Valence Band). The upper 
band remains completely empty (the Conduction Band).
