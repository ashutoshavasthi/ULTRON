MOS Transistor Basics
I. Introduction to CMOS and Digital Design
The study of CMOS digital VLSI design focuses primarily on creating electronic circuits based on
complementary metal oxide semiconductors.
1. CMOS Definition: CMOS stands for Complementary Metal Oxide Semiconductor.
2. Basic Building Block: The foundational component used in CMOS digital design is the MOSFET,
which stands for Metal Oxide Semiconductor Field Effect Transistor.
3. Digital Logic vs. Analog:
Digital Logic: You work strictly with only zero’s (0) and one’s (1).
0 usually represents the ground (GND).
1 usually represents the applied voltage (VDD).
Analog Design: Signals are time dependent (like a sine wave 
). In analog design, the
behavior of the signal must be known at multiple points in time.
4. The Goal: Digital VLSI design requires devices that can switch easily between 0 and 1 states at very
high speeds and with relatively low power dissipation.
II. MOSFET Function: Working as a Switch
The primary motivation for studying the MOSFET is its ability to act as an efficient switch.
1. In a MOSFET, by applying a suitable electrical potential on the Gate terminal, you can manipulate
(regulate) the amount of current flowing from the Source to the Drain.
2. Field Effect: The MOSFET is a Field Effect Transistor because the movement (flow) of electrons
within the device is controlled by the electric field that is present.
3. Switching States:
ON State: The MOSFET is conducting current (implying a ‘1’ or high voltage).
OFF State: The MOSFET is not conducting current (implying a ‘0’ or ground).
III. MOSFET Structure and Terminals
The MOSFET is fundamentally a multi-terminal device.
1. Terminals:
It is primarily considered a three-terminal device: Source (S), Drain (D), and Gate (G).
It effectively has four terminals, with the fourth being the Substrate Contact (or Bulk Contact).
2. Physical Layers (The MOS Structure):
The term MOS (Metal Oxide Semiconductor) refers to the layers that form the control structure.
M (Metal/Gate): The top layer, often made of metal or polysilicon (which acts almost like a
metal), is used for the Gate terminal.
O (Oxide): A layer of silicon dioxide sits beneath the gate. It acts as an insulator.
S (Semiconductor/Substrate): The foundation of the device, often a P-type
semiconductor substrate (for an NMOS device).

V. Types of MOSFETs
MOSFETs can be categorized based on their charge carriers and their basic operational mode.
A. Based on Charge Carriers:
1. NMOS (N-type MOSFET): Electrons are the primary charge carriers.

Requires negative gate and drain voltages.
Uses an N-type substrate with P+ Source/Drain regions.

1. Enhancement Mode MOSFET:
"Normally Off" Device: Under zero bias conditions (no voltage applied to any terminal), there is
no conducting channel between the source and drain, meaning there is no current flow.
A voltage must be applied to the gate to “enhance” the formation of a channel before current
can flow.
2. Depletion Mode MOSFET:
"Normally On" Device: Even under zero bias conditions, a conducting channel is already present
between source and drain.
Current will flow as soon as a drain voltage is applied.
Note: In this course, we primarily focus on the Enhancement Mode MOSFET.
VI. The Threshold Voltage (
) and Channel Formation
The core concept in controlling a MOSFET is understanding the formation of the conducting channel, which
is governed by the Threshold Voltage.
1. Threshold Voltage Definition: 
 is defined as the specific gate voltage at which the channel is
formed or the channel is inverted.
2. Cut-Off Mode (VG = 0): When the gate voltage is zero, the P-type substrate beneath the oxide has a
large quantity of free charge carriers (holes). There is no path (channel) for electrons to move from
source to drain, resulting in no current flow.
3. Inversion (Channel Formation in NMOS):
Applying Positive VG: As a positive gate voltage is applied, an electric field is created.
This positive field repels the holes in the P-substrate away from the oxide interface and attracts
electrons (minority carriers) toward the interface.
Depletion: Initially, the holes are pushed away, leaving behind a depletion region containing
only fixed negative ions.
Inversion: When the gate voltage reaches 
, enough electrons accumulate near the surface
to match the concentration of holes that were originally there. This creates an inversion layer
(an N-type channel).
Once the channel is formed, applying a small drain voltage will cause electrons to be dragged
from source to drain, resulting in current flow.

properties and doping concentration used during fabrication. For a finished device,

considered fixed.
