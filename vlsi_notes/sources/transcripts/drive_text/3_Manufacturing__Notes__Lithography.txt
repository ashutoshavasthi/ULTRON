Photolithography in IC Fabrication
I. Photolithography
Photolithography is a crucial technique used for printing extremely small patterns onto a semiconductor
wafer. It is central to the manufacturing of integrated circuits (ICs).
2. The Process Flow (Overview)
The process involves several steps used hundreds of times until a complete chip is finished:
1. Film Deposition: A film layer is deposited onto the substrate (wafer).
2. Photoresist Application: A layer of photosensitive material, called resist, is applied on top of the film.
3. Exposure: The pattern of the chip, contained on a mask, is transferred into the resist by exposure to
light using a scanner.
4. Development: The exposed areas of the resist are treated. In the process shown in the sources, the
exposed resist becomes insoluble, and the unexposed areas are removed. This leaves a replica of the
desired pattern.
5. Etching/Implantation: The patterned resist acts as a mask for subsequent steps, such as ion
implantation, barrier formation, or the application and polishing of materials like copper interconnect.
6. Resist Removal: Once the underlying film has been patterned, the remaining resist is removed.
3. Importance in IC Fabrication
Photolithography enables the miniaturization of ICs, leading to ever more powerful devices that
continuously change our world.
Moore’s Law: The hard work of tens of thousands of engineers in scaling features has realized
Moore’s Law, an economic principle posed in 1965 stating that the amount of transistors per given
area will double approximately every two years at similar costs.
Scaling Complexity: Modern chips exhibit staggering complexity. The first commercial monolithic IC
(1961) had four transistors, while the Nvidia Hopper chip (2022) contained 80 billion transistors.
Societal Impact: This miniaturization has driven major shifts, such as making personalized medicine
affordable (due to massive cost reductions in genome mapping) and enabling the electrification of the
automotive industry.
II. Photoresists: Positive vs. Negative
Positive and negative photoresists differ in how they respond to ultraviolet (UV) light during
photolithography. In a positive photoresist, the regions exposed to UV light become chemically weaker and
more soluble in the developer, so the exposed areas are removed, and the final pattern on the wafer is the
same as the mask pattern. This type of resist offers higher resolution, better dimensional control, and
improved alignment accuracy, making it suitable for advanced and fine-feature IC fabrication. In contrast, a
negative photoresist undergoes polymer cross-linking when exposed to UV light, causing the exposed

B. Techniques to Enhance Feature Size Capability
1. Decreasing Wavelength ( )
The industry roadmap has historically followed a path of decreasing wavelength to improve resolution:
Shift from Broad Spectrum to Narrowband: Early systems used mercury arc lamps (broad spectrum).
Because lenses struggle to focus multiple wavelengths simultaneously (leading to chromatic
aberration and blurred patterns), the industry shifted to narrowband Excimer lasers.
Deep Ultraviolet (DUV) Light Sources: These massive machines (roughly the size of three
refrigerators) produce deep ultraviolet light (e.g., 193 nm wavelength). They generate light by exciting
Argon-fluoride gas with electrical pulses, which is then filtered, amplified, and stretched into a
continuous, stable beam.
Extreme Ultraviolet (EUV): For the most advanced critical layers, EUV uses extremely short
wavelengths of 13.5 nm. This light is generated by launching molten droplets of tin into a vacuum,
hitting them with an infrared laser to create superheated tin plasma, which then emits the 13.5 nm
light.

manufacturing of high-quality aspherical lens elements enabled further increases in NA.

3. Reducing the Process Factor (
)
Computational Lithography and OPC: This powerful process involves iteratively modifying the mask
patterns and illumination source shapes to achieve the best imaging performance.
Optical Proximity Correction (OPC): This involves predistorting the mask layout so that after
the imaging process (which has finite resolution), the image printed on the wafer is close to the
original desired layout. This distortion can involve modifying each polygon segment. Free form
OPC is the latest innovation, where the shapes on the mask may bear little resemblance to the
desired shapes on the wafer.
OPC can also insert extra mask patterns too small to print, which helps reduce the impact of
manufacturing variations like slight changes in focus.
Advanced Illumination Techniques: By changing the shape of the light source, engineers manage
diffraction and interference to improve image contrast.
Examples include annular illumination and dipole illumination, which remove parts of the
illumination pupil that do not contribute to constructive interference.
The Flexay (an array of electronically programmable micromirrors) allows scanners to create
illumination patterns of arbitrary complexity.
Other Resolution Enhancement Techniques (RETs): Strategies like Phase-shift masks and Multiple
patterning are also used to enhance resolution.
