1. The Crystalline Structure and Energy Bands 
Crystalline Material 
A material where atoms are arranged periodically in all directions 
(x, y, z). The arrangement must look uniform and the same in any 
position of the lattice. Only crystalline materials technically allow 
for the definition of a band gap. 
Energy Band 
Formation 
As individual atoms are brought close together, their discrete 
energy levels (e.g., 2s, 2p levels) split up, eventually forming 
continuous energy bands. 
Energy Band Gap 
(E_g) 
The energy difference between the highest filled band (valence 
band) and the empty band (conduction band). 
Valence Band (VB) 
This band is fully filled with electrons. Electrons in a completely 
filled band cannot carry current because they lack empty states 
to move into. 
Conduction Band (CB) 
This band is typically empty. When electrons are excited from the 
VB to the CB, they can flow under the application of an electric 
field, thus carrying current. 
Current Flow 
Current can flow only when electrons are excited to the CB, or 
when empty states (created by the excited electrons) are present 
in the VB.

3. Electron Volt and Thermal Energy 
A. The Electron Volt (eV) 
The unit used to measure energy 
 
B. Thermal Energy 
At any temperature above 0 Kelvin (such as room temperature), thermal energy is present, 
causing atoms to vibrate. 
 
Calculation at Room Temperature (T ≈ 300 K): 
1. Boltzmann constant (k): 1.38 × 10⁻²³ SI unit. 
2. Thermal energy in Joules: k × T = (1.38 × 10⁻²³) × 300. 
3. Converting to eV (by dividing by 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹): 
kT ≈ 0.0259 eV ≈ 26 milli electron volt (meV) 
 

--- page 3 ---
 
Significance: This thermal energy is very small compared to the silicon band gap (1.1 eV). 
Despite the low thermal energy, electrons can still be excited from VB to CB, a process that 
depends on the statistical distribution of carriers.

4. Reciprocal Space and the E-k Diagram 
 
To understand electron movement in crystals, we shift from real space (distance) to 
reciprocal space (momentum). 
Concept 
Formula / Description 
Wave Vector (k) 
k is a reciprocal space vector, representing 
momentum direction. 
Momentum (p) 
p = ħk (where ħ = h / 2π) 
E-k Relation (Free Space) 
E = ħ²k² / 2m 
 
A. Wave Vector (k) and Momentum (p) 
1. Reciprocal Space: The Fourier transform of real space (distance). Its unit is 1/distance (e.g., 
1/centimeter). 
2. Wave Vector (k): Defined mathematically as: k = 2π / λ Where λ is the wavelength 
(electrons are treated as both particles and waves). This quantity k represents the 
momentum of the electron. 
 
3. Reduced Planck Constant (ħ): ħ = h / 2π (where h is Planck's constant). 
4. Momentum Formula (De Broglie): p = ħk 

--- page 4 ---
 This equation shows that the wave vector k is directly proportional to the electron 
momentum p. 
 
B. Energy and Momentum Relation (Free Electron) 
The kinetic energy (E) of a particle is related to its momentum (p) and mass (m) by E = p² / 
2m. 
Formula for Kinetic Energy (E-k relation):  
 
If Energy (E) is plotted against the wave vector (k), the resulting graph is a parabola (E ∝ k²). 
This parabolic relation is only true for a free electron moving in a vacuum. 
• E-k Diagram: A plot of Energy (E) on the y-axis versus Wave Vector (k) on the x-axis, 
showing a parabolic shape. 
 
5. Electrons in a Real Crystal and Effective Mass 
A. Effect of crystal lattice  
1. Periodic Potential: In a crystal, the electrons are not free. The periodic arrangement of 
atoms introduces a periodic potential. 
2. Quantum Mechanical Solution: To determine the motion of the electron, Schrödinger’s 
equation must be solved. 

--- page 5 ---
3. E-k Diagram Distortion: Due to the interaction with the periodic potential, the E-k 
diagram becomes distorted. 
4. Band Gaps in E-k Space: The distortion leads to discontinuities or gaps. These appear 
periodically at integer multiples of π/a (where a is the lattice constant). 
 
B. The First Brillouin Zone 
 
• Range of k from -π/a to +π/a. 
• Device physics and electron transport are explained within this zone. 
• Near k=0, the relation is approximately parabolic, where most electrons occupy low energy 
states. 
 C. Effective Mass (m*) 
A. Definition and Formula 
• Effective Mass (m*): The mass an electron experiences while moving inside the periodic 
potential of a semiconductor crystal. It is generally different from the free electron mass (m₀). 
• Near k=0, the E-k relation still holds, provided m is replaced by m*: E = ħ²k² / 2m* 
• Formula for Effective Mass (Derived from Curvature): The effective mass is mathematically 
defined as the inverse of the second derivative of the E-k relation, multiplied by ħ²: 
m* = ħ² / (d²E / dk²) 
The term d²E / dk² represents the curvature of the E-k plot. 

--- page 6 ---
B. Significance and Role of Effective Mass 
1. Condensation of Quantum Mechanics: m* takes into account the entire band structure 
information of the material (which comes from solving the complex Schrödinger equation). 
2. Allows Newton’s law (F = ma) to be applied to electrons. 
3. Transport Dependence (Curvature and Mobility): Higher curvature → lower m* → higher 
mobility. 
   ◦ m* is inversely proportional to the curvature of the E-k diagram. 
   ◦ A material with higher curvature has a lower effective mass. 
   ◦ A lower effective mass leads to higher electron mobility. 
   ◦ Higher mobility is desirable as it results in higher current and superior transport for high-
speed devices. 
 
6. The Concept of Holes (Quasi-Particles) 
Holes are as important as electrons in semiconductor research. 
A. Definition and Formation 
1. Valence Band (VB) and Conduction Band (CB): The VB is completely filled with 
electrons. Because it is filled, electrons cannot move and carry current. The CB is nearly 
empty. 
2. Excitation: When an electron is excited from the VB to the CB (e.g., by heat or light), it is 
free to move and carry current. 
3. Hole as a Vacancy: The excited electron leaves behind a vacancy (an empty space) in the 
VB. This vacancy is called a hole. 

--- page 7 ---
4. Hole Definition: A hole is essentially the absence of an electron. 
B. Modeling and Transport 
1. Quasi-Particle: Although a hole is an absence of an electron, for convenience in 
semiconductor device analysis, it is modeled as a particle (a quasi-particle). 
2. Current Flow: When an electric field is applied, nearby electrons in the VB jump into the 
empty hole space. 
3. Charge and Direction: 
   ◦ Electrons move opposite to the electric field direction. 
   ◦ Due to the sequential jumping of electrons, the hole (the empty space) appears to move 
in the same direction as the electric field. 
   ◦ Therefore, the hole is modeled as having a positive charge. 
4. Energy Band: Holes move in the Valence Band. 
7. E-K Diagram for Holes (Band Structure) 
A. Hole E-K Diagram and Effective Mass 
1. Hole Energy: Holes have their own E-K diagram which corresponds to the Valence Band.  
2. Hole Effective Mass (mₕ*): Holes have their own effective masses. This mass is calculated 
from the inverse of the curvature of the hole E-K diagram. 
   mₕ* ∝ 1 / (d²E / dk²) 
3. Band Structure vs. Band Diagram: 
   ◦ The overall E-K plot showing both electron (CB) and hole (VB) bands is called the band 
structure E-K diagram. 
   ◦ The simple plot of Energy vs. real space (distance) is called the band diagram.

8. Direct vs. Indirect Bandgap Semiconductors 
A. Energy Band Gap (Eg) 
The energy band gap (Eg) is defined as the smallest energy separation between the highest 
point of the Valence Band and the lowest point of the Conduction Band. 
 
B. Direct Bandgap Semiconductors 
1. Definition: The bottommost point of the Conduction Band and the topmost point of the 
Valence Band occur at the same K value (often K=0). 
2. Momentum Transition: Excitation or recombination requires no change in momentum (ΔK 
= 0). 
3. Efficiency: This is a very efficient process. 
4. Applications: These materials can efficiently emit light (used for LEDs) because electrons 
and holes can recombine directly, releasing a photon. They also have a very high absorption 
coefficient for light. 

--- page 9 ---
5. Examples: Gallium Arsenide (GaAs), Gallium Nitride (GaN). 
C. Indirect Bandgap Semiconductors 
1. Definition: The lowest point of the CB and the highest point of the VB are not at the same K 
value (ΔK ≠ 0). 
2. Momentum Transition: A transition requires a large change in momentum (ΔK). 
3. Phonon Assistance: This transition is inefficient and requires the assistance of atomic 
vibrations (called phonons) to change the momentum. 
4. Applications: These materials cannot efficiently emit light (because the energy is often 
given to atomic vibrations instead of a photon). While absorption is possible (e.g., silicon solar 
cells), the absorption coefficient is much lower. 
5. Examples: Silicon (Si), Germanium (Ge). 
9. Fermi-Dirac Statistics and Probability 
A. The Fermi Function 
The probability that an energy state Eᵢ is occupied by an electron is given by the Fermi 
function, f(E): 
f(Eᵢ) = 1 / (1 + exp((Eᵢ – E_F) / (k_B T))) 
• E_F: Fermi level (or Fermi energy). 
• k_B: Boltzmann constant (not the momentum vector k). 
• T: Temperature in Kelvin. 
 

--- page 10 ---
B. The Fermi Level (E_F) 
1. Definition: The Fermi level is a statistical construct. 
2. Statistical Property: It is the energy level where the probability of finding an electron is 
exactly 50% (0.5). 
   ◦ This 50% probability holds true at any temperature. 
3. Importance: The Fermi level is the most important concept for semiconductor device 
analysis. 
C. Electron and Hole Probability 
1. Electron Probability: f(E) is the probability of finding an electron. 
2. Hole Probability: 1 - f(E) is the probability of finding a hole (or the probability of a state 
being empty).
