Semiconductor Doping Techniques
These notes cover the methods and physics used to introduce dopants into semiconductors, focusing on
both traditional Thermal Diffusion and modern Ion Implantation.
I. Importance of Doping Control in Semiconductors
Doping is essential for creating semiconductor devices. Precise control over the concentration and
distribution of dopants is critical.
Control Required: Doping must be controlled as a function of vertical depth into the wafer and
lateral position across the wafer.
Device Impact: The doping profile significantly affects device characteristics, particularly for
MOSFETs (Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistors).
Parasitic Resistance: Source and drain doping concentration and depth control drain and source
resistance. High resistance wastes applied voltage and lowers the device drive current, meaning
lower resistance leads to higher device performance.
Short Channel Effects: The doping profile in the channel impacts short channel behavior, such
as punch-through.
Scaling Challenge: As transistor dimensions are scaled down (following Moore’s Law), controlling
doping becomes increasingly challenging, especially in advanced 3D structures like FinFETs and
nanosheet transistors.
II. Thermal Diffusion (Historical Doping Method)
Thermal diffusion was historically used for doping semiconductor devices in the early days of
microelectronics (1960s–1980s). While often supplanted by ion implantation now, the underlying physics
remains relevant.
A. Process and Hardware
Process: Involves making dopants available in a furnace at high temperatures and driving them into
the silicon wafer.
Hardware: Diffusion uses horizontal quartz furnace tubes, often called oxidation diffusion furnaces,
surrounded by heating coils.
Operation: Wafers are loaded vertically and slowly, and the temperature is ramped up to the diffusion
temperature (often 800–

B. Lateral Control (Masking)
If wafers are bare, doping occurs everywhere on the surface, allowing control only as a function of
depth.
Lateral control requires a mask. The most common mask is silicon dioxide (
).
Why 
 Works: The diffusivity of common dopants (phosphorus, arsenic, boron) is much lower in

temperature), dopants will not penetrate the mask, entering the silicon only through etched windows.
C. Physics of Diffusion (Fick’s Laws)
Mechanism: Diffusion involves the random, statistical movement (Brownian motion) of dopants from
a high-concentration region (the surface) to a low-concentration region (the wafer bulk).

III. Ion Implantation (Modern Doping Method)
Ion implantation supplanted thermal diffusion around the 1980s because it offers the precise control over
doping profiles necessary for advanced devices.
A. Advantages and Profile
Precision: Provides very precise control over doping concentration.
Temperature: It is generally a low-temperature process compared to diffusion.
Masking: Allows a wide choice of masking materials (metals, thick 
, or photoresist).
Profile Shape: Unlike diffusion (where concentration peaks at the surface), ion implantation achieves a
maximum concentration at a certain depth beneath the surface.
B. Ion Implantation System
The system involves accelerating ions in a linear accelerator and slamming them into the wafer. The process
typically follows these major steps:
1. Dopant Gas 
 Ionizer: Dopant materials (gases or solids) are ionized by bombarding them with high-
energy electrons or using a plasma discharge, creating charged ions (e.g., 
).
2. Mass Separator: Uses an analyzing magnet to deflect the ion beam. Since different ions have
different masses and charges, the magnetic field separates them, allowing only the desired ion species
to pass through a slit.
3. Accelerator: Increases the selected ion beam’s energy. This energy determines the depth of
penetration into the wafer.
4. Scanning and Focusing: Mechanisms focus the beam and scanners (X and Y axes) use electrostatic
potential to deflect the charged beam, controlling the position where the doping occurs on the wafer.
5. Neutral Beam Trapping: Ions can become neutral during travel. Neutral atoms cannot be controlled or
deflected by the electrostatic scanners and would dope the wafer unpredictably. These neutral beams
are trapped, and only the charged ions are allowed to proceed to the wafer.
6. Dose Measurement: The charged ion beam current is measured using a Faraday cup (an integrator
circuit) to accurately determine the actual dose implanted into the wafer.
